Is it worth it?

How is this different from other sleep consultants?

Three things. The plan is written for your baby by Dr. Sam, not assembled from an age template. Support is daily and 1:1, with a coach live on Night 1, rather than a handful of scheduled calls with days of guesswork in between. And the outcome is guaranteed with a refund. How is this different from a course or a book?

Courses and books usually contain sound information; the difficulty is applying it to one specific baby at 3 a.m. A course cannot see that your baby’s wake window is off by 30 minutes or tell you whether tonight is a night to hold the line. If you have not yet tried one consistent approach, a course is a reasonable first step. If you have and it did not hold, the missing piece is usually individual fitting and daily accountability.

Can’t I just get this from ChatGPT or another AI assistant?

AI assistants are good at explaining how baby sleep works and what the common methods are. What they cannot do is read your baby’s log every morning, keep one plan steady across two weeks, notice the pattern you are too tired to see, and be accountable for the result. Many families arrive after trying to piece a plan together at night from many sources; the plan was rarely the problem, the consistency was.

Shouldn’t I just try it on my own first?

You can, and many babies respond to one approach followed consistently for a week. The risk is the partial attempt: putting the baby down awake, then rescuing after a long protest, teaches the baby that protesting long enough works, and makes the next attempt harder. If you try on your own, choose one approach and do not change it mid-week. If you already have and it fell apart, that is the point where coaching earns its place.

The method

Is this the cry-it-out method?

No. Dr. Sam is a parent too and is specific about this: the team would never simply leave a baby to cry and figure it out. Most of the work happens before the baby goes into the crib, so that the baby understands what is being asked and protests less. Because a baby is learning a new skill, zero tears is not a realistic promise for anyone, but the goal is the least crying overall: typically one harder night, then falling asleep faster each night after.

How much crying should I expect?

It depends on age and habits, so no honest coach will give a number. The usual pattern: Night 1 is the learning night and may involve some protest; from Night 2 the time to fall asleep is typically cut in half; within a few nights most babies settle within minutes for both bedtime and naps. Younger babies with fewer habits generally protest least.

Will sleep training hurt my baby’s attachment?

The research says no. Randomized trials of behavioral sleep training, including a five-year follow-up, found no differences in attachment, emotional health or stress hormones between trained and untrained children. What parents report is the opposite of harm: a rested baby is happier during the day and a rested parent is more present. See Is sleep training safe?

Will my baby feel abandoned?

No. A baby who is set up well during the day, put down by a calm parent, and responded to in a consistent way is not being abandoned; they are being taught. Most babies in the program are going to sleep contentedly in their crib within a few days, and wake happy in the morning.

What if my baby throws up from crying?

This is uncommon. If your baby is prone to it, say so at intake: the plan will include exactly how to respond calmly without undoing progress. Vomiting linked to illness, reflux or feeding is a medical question for your pediatrician, and sleep training pauses during a significant illness.

What is the worst case scenario?

That you follow the plan consistently and correctly and your baby still does not sleep 8–12 hours within 14 days. In that case you receive a full refund; it is in the Terms of Service you sign. The realistic downside is a few focused days of effort.

My baby

What types of babies does this work for?

Actually Sleeping works with babies and toddlers from 10 weeks to 3 years, including reflux babies, NICU graduates, twins and triplets, co-sleepers, contact nappers, babies fed or rocked to sleep, and toddlers in beds. The deciding factor is rarely the baby. It is whether the parents are ready to follow one plan for the first few nights, which is what the consultation is for.

Is my baby too young?

Formal sleep training suits most babies from around 4 months, but good foundations can start earlier and are far gentler, because there is no habit to undo and night feeds stay in place. We work with babies from 10 weeks. For babies younger than that, the guarantee clock starts on the 10-week birthday. Your pediatrician decides anything about feeding and weight gain.

Is my baby too old to sleep train?

No. Older babies and toddlers often learn fastest, because they understand quickly once the message is consistent. The same intelligence means they will test any inconsistency, so clarity matters more at 12 or 18 months than at 5. Many of the reviews describing 11- and 12-hour nights within the first two nights are from babies over a year old.

What if my baby is extra sensitive?

Sensitive babies benefit most from consistency, because mixed signals are what prolong crying. A plan paced for your baby’s temperament, held steady, is gentler on a sensitive baby than weeks of trying different things.

What if my baby is strong-willed?

Strong-willed babies are common and the plan accounts for it. Persistence cuts both ways: a determined baby protests harder against confusion, and learns faster once the routine is unmistakable. Consistency from both parents is what matters.

Can you help reflux or NICU babies?

Yes, with adjustments built into the plan: feed timing, time upright before sleep, and pacing. Reflux should be managed with your pediatrician first, and premature babies are assessed by corrected age. Safe sleep does not change: flat, on the back, no inclines. See sleep training a baby with reflux.

Can you help with naps too?

Yes. Naps usually fall into place within days of nights being solved, and they are coached with no time limit until they are solid. That is part of every plan, not an add-on.

What if my baby only contact naps?

Contact naps are lovely when they are a choice. The plan teaches your baby to nap in the crib so that holding them for a nap becomes something you do when you want to, not the only way a nap happens. See how to stop contact napping.

What if my baby can stand, sit, or pull up in the crib?

That is normal for most of the babies we work with and does not get in the way. Practise getting down from standing during the day; at night the response stays the same. Mobile babies are old enough to understand a consistent routine quickly.

What if my baby is going through a regression?

The change around 4 months is a permanent shift in how sleep is organized, so there is nothing to wait out. Later “regressions” are usually developmental phases layered on a baby who needs help falling asleep. Babies who can fall asleep independently typically ride those phases out in a few nights.

Sleep setup

Can I still co-sleep?

During the program your baby sleeps in their own crib or bassinet, so the message is clear. That is also what the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends for infants, and it advises against bed-sharing in the first year. Most families who come to us were bed-sharing out of exhaustion rather than choice.

What if my baby sleeps in our room?

Room-sharing is fine and is what the AAP recommends for the first 6 months. The plan works with a crib or bassinet in your room; families have done it with relatives staying in the same room.

Can you help us transition out of our bed?

Yes. The large majority of families who contact us are bed-sharing when they start. Moving a baby from the parents’ bed to their own sleep space is one of the most common things the plan covers.

Can you help us transition from bassinet to crib?

Yes. Done as part of a plan, the move is usually easier than parents expect, because the baby is learning to fall asleep independently at the same time and the skill travels with them.

What about the Snoo or a smart bassinet?

Whether to use one is a personal choice, and some young babies settle well in them. A motion bassinet does the soothing for the baby, so the skill of falling asleep independently still has to be learned when the baby outgrows it. We help families make that transition.

My household

Does my partner really have to be on the consultation?

Yes, we ask that both parents or all decision-makers attend. Because results are guaranteed, the team needs to hear from everyone who will be putting the baby to bed before it can judge honestly whether it can help, and a plan only works when both adults follow it the same way.

What if my spouse or partner disagrees about sleep training?

This is common, and often follows a previous attempt that went badly. A clear written plan, a neutral coach, and visible progress within the first night or two usually settle it. The skeptical parent frequently becomes the most committed one.

What if I’m a single parent?

Single parents do very well. One adult means one consistent response, and daily contact with a coach replaces the second pair of hands at decision points.

Will grandparents be able to follow this?

Yes. Once your baby has the skill, other caregivers only need a few simple pointers, which you will be able to hand over on one page.

Can you help with my other kids as well?

Yes. Tell us on the consultation how many children you want help with and their ages; plans regularly cover an older sibling alongside a baby.

Can you help twins?

Yes, twins and triplets. Multiples are used to a noisier room and generally adapt quickly when kept on the same schedule. See sleep training twins.

What about nannies, daycares and other caregivers?

A sleep plan has to work in real life, not only at home with one parent. After the first few nights the routine comes down to a few clear steps that a nanny, grandparent or daycare can follow, and your coach will help you brief them.

How does this work with daycare?

Daycare naps are rarely perfect and do not need to be. The plan protects night sleep, tells you how to adjust bedtime after a short-nap day, and gives you a short note for the daycare.

Can this work if I’m back at work?

Yes. Most clients work. Check-ins are by message, not scheduled calls, and the routine is designed to be handed to whoever is with your baby during the day.

Timing and life

What if we don’t have a consistent schedule right now?

That is where most families start. Building the schedule is part of the plan, not a prerequisite.

What if bedtime is different every night?

Bedtime has some flexibility once the days are set up well. A short, repeatable routine matters more than a fixed minute on the clock.

Can we start if my baby is teething or sick?

Mild congestion or teething is not a reason to wait. Fever, vomiting or a significant illness is: pause, see your pediatrician, and start when your baby is well.

What happens if my baby gets sick after sleep training?

Babies who can fall asleep independently usually keep sleeping well through minor illness, because their bodies want the rest. Comfort a sick baby as needed, then return to the normal routine when they recover; your coach will tell you how.

What if travel, a move, or a big life event is coming up?

A baby who has the skill generally carries it through travel and change, and your coach will plan around the dates. If a trip falls in the first few days, it is usually better to start right after.

What happens after the first 7 days?

The first week is the intensive part, and by the end of it most babies have slept through several times. After that, coaching continues: refining wake windows and bedtime, coaching naps until they are solid, and making sure you know what to watch for so the results last. A baby graduates after seven sleep-throughs and then 14 or more consecutive nights.
