Can I sleep train while room-sharing?

Yes. The AAP recommends that parents sleep in the same room as their baby, but not in the same bed, preferably for at least the first six months, and you can teach independent sleep in that arrangement. It is usually harder, because your baby can see, hear and smell you. A plan written for a shared room makes it workable.

Room-sharing is not bed-sharing

Room-sharing means your baby sleeps in a crib or bassinet in your room: on the back, on a firm, flat, non-inclined surface, with no soft toys, pillows, blankets or bedding. The AAP recommends room-sharing and says it cannot support bed-sharing under any circumstances. It notes the risk is higher on couches and soft armchairs, so never let your baby sleep on a sofa or armchair. Do not use inclined sleepers, wedges or positioners, and never drape fabric over or on the crib.

Why it is harder

  • Babies stir between sleep cycles, see or smell a parent, and call for the usual help.
  • Parents hear every grunt and respond before the baby has fully woken. Babies are noisy in light sleep.
  • Parents’ coughing, phones and movement wake the baby, particularly in the early morning hours.
  • Many general methods assume you can leave the room. In a shared room you cannot, which changes how almost every response works.

This is a common situation for families who come to Actually Sleeping, including families in apartments where a separate room is not an option. Dr. Sam writes the plan for your actual room, so the response fits the space you have.

Why the setup matters

In a shared room, the distance between beds, what your baby can see from the crib, light at the window and how sound carries all affect how often your baby wakes and what they expect when they do. Getting those right, safely, is part of the plan. Anything you add to the room has to respect safe sleep: nothing in or over the crib, and any divider or screen well away from it.

Why general methods stall in a shared room

  • Leave-the-room methods do not translate directly to 3 a.m. when you are sleeping a few feet away.
  • Parent-present methods fit better, but your presence all night means there is no point where you withdraw.
  • Early mornings are hardest, because your own waking and movement start the day early for your baby.

After 6 months

Moving a baby to their own room after 6 months is a family decision. Many families room-share far longer by choice or necessity, and independent sleep can be taught either way. The same principles apply to siblings sharing a room; see sleep training twins. General safety points are in safe sleep and sleep training.

Frequently asked

Can you sleep train a baby in the same room as you?

Yes. It usually takes a plan adapted to the shared room, because methods that rely on leaving the room do not translate directly to the middle of the night. Actually Sleeping writes plans for shared rooms, including one-bedroom homes.

Should I move my baby to their own room to sleep train?

Not before 6 months unless you have discussed it with your pediatrician; the AAP recommends room-sharing preferably for at least the first six months. After that it is your choice, and independent sleep can be taught either way.

Does room-sharing cause more night wakings?

It can, because babies and parents disturb each other, especially in light sleep and early in the morning. Room setup and independent sleep skills make a large difference.

Can I sleep train in a one-bedroom apartment?

Yes. Many families do. The plan has to account for the room, the distance between beds and early mornings. Dr. Sam writes Actually Sleeping plans for the space you actually have, and the written guarantee applies.
