My toddler says they’re scared of the dark. How do I help?

Take the fear seriously and keep bedtime the same. Offer a dim night light, a small comfort object if your child is over 12 months, and calm reassurance that they are safe. Avoid scary shows and books before bed, and keep check-ins brief and predictable. If fears are severe, last for weeks, or show up during the day, talk with your pediatrician.

Why it starts

As toddlers’ imaginations grow, so does their ability to picture things that aren’t there. Many children go through a stage of being afraid of the dark, monsters or noises at night. It is a normal part of development, not a sign that something is wrong.

It can be hard to tell real fear from stalling. A frightened child usually looks scared: clinging, crying, tense, and wanting you close. A stalling child tends to have a new request each time. Some children do both.

What helps

  1. Listen and name it. “You feel scared when it’s dark. That’s okay. You are safe here.” Don’t laugh at the fear or tell them it’s silly.
  2. A dim night light. The AAP says you can leave a light on if it helps a child after a nightmare. Keep it dim and low; bright evening light can suppress melatonin in young children.
  3. A comfort object. For children over 12 months, a small lovey can help. The AAP’s parent site says most experts agree soft objects pose little risk to healthy babies after 12 months.
  4. Calm content before bed. The AAP advises no screens in the hour before bed and choosing calm programs over intense ones. Scary books and shows can feed fears at night.
  5. Daytime practice. Play with flashlights, make shadow puppets, or have a picnic in a dark blanket fort. Getting comfortable with the dark during play helps at night.
  6. Brief, predictable check-ins. Tell your child you will come back to check on them in a few minutes, then do it. Keep each visit short and calm, and space them out.

What tends to backfire

  • Dismissing it. “There’s nothing to be scared of” can leave a child feeling unheard.
  • Elaborate monster rituals. Searching under the bed every night can suggest there is something to find. A simple “I checked the room before bed and it’s safe” is enough.
  • Staying until they fall asleep every night, or bringing them into your bed. These can help in a rough patch, but they quickly become what your child needs to fall asleep.
  • A long bedtime. The longer the routine, the more time there is to think about the dark. Keep it short and warm.

Nightmares

If your child wakes up scared, remembers a bad dream, and wants comfort, that is a nightmare. The AAP advises comforting your child quickly, reassuring them they are safe, and reminding them that dreams aren’t real. Night terrors are different: your child is not really awake. See nightmares vs night terrors.

When to get help

Talk with your pediatrician if the fears are intense, last for weeks without improving, appear during the day too, or come with other changes such as trouble eating, frequent tantrums, or loss of skills. Sometimes a stressful event, such as a move or a new sibling, is behind a new fear; see a new sibling and moving house.

Frequently asked

Should I leave the light on for my scared toddler?

A dim night light is fine and can help. Keep it low and out of your child’s direct view. A bright overhead light can interfere with sleep, so avoid leaving the main light on.

Is it okay to stay with my toddler until they fall asleep?

Occasionally, during a hard week, yes. Doing it every night can make your presence part of how your child falls asleep. Brief, predictable check-ins tend to work better over time and build confidence.

Should I check under the bed for monsters?

A quick, matter-of-fact “I checked your room and it’s safe” is enough. Nightly searches or monster sprays can suggest there is something to find. Calm reassurance and a dim light usually work better.
