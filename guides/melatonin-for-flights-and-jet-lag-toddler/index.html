Is it safe to give a baby or toddler melatonin for a flight or jet lag?

Not without your pediatrician’s direction. The AAP says melatonin should only be used after a discussion with your pediatrician. It is sold as a supplement, product strength varies widely, and child poisonings rose sharply over the past decade. Light, meals and naps on local time shift a young child’s body clock without a supplement.

A note on scope: Dr. Sam is a Doctor of Pharmacy and a licensed pharmacist, not a physician. This page explains in general terms how a medicine, supplement or remedy relates to sleep; it does not replace your child’s pediatrician. Check with your child’s pediatrician before giving your child any medicine, supplement or remedy, and follow the product label.

The short answer

A one-off dose for travel can sound harmless. The problems are the same as with nightly use: you cannot be sure how much is in the product, there is little research in babies and toddlers, and travel bags are where curious toddlers find gummies. For a young child, planning the trip around light and schedule avoids those problems.

What the evidence and the regulators say

  • Pediatrician first. The AAP says melatonin should only be used after a discussion with your pediatrician. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine advises parents to talk with a health care professional before giving melatonin or any supplement to children.
  • Unregulated strength. Melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement. The AASM cites a study in which melatonin content ranged from less than half to more than four times the amount on the label, with chewables varying most. CDC travel guidance also notes melatonin is not regulated by the FDA.
  • Poisonings. A CDC report found pediatric melatonin ingestions rose about 530% from 2012 to 2021. Children 5 and younger made up most reports, and two children under 2 died.
  • Jet lag benefit does not settle safety. NIH notes melatonin may help with jet lag, but its long-term safety has not been established.
  • No help for ears. CDC guidance says antihistamines are contraindicated under age 1 and have not been shown to help ear pain on flights; swallowing does. Let babies nurse or suck a bottle or pacifier on descent.

How the body clock actually shifts

CDC travel guidance gives an average adaptation of about 1.5 hours a day after flying west and 1 hour a day after flying east. Bright morning light helps the clock move earlier; evening light pushes it later. CDC also notes jet lag disturbs children’s sleep just as it does adults’. So the tools are the same for a toddler: light, meals and sleep timing.

A travel plan that does not need a supplement

  • On the plane: any safe sleep counts. Feeding, holding and the motion all help.
  • On arrival: get outside in daylight in the local morning, and eat on local time.
  • Naps: keep them, but cap long ones so the night stays long.
  • Evenings: dim the room an hour before local bedtime and run your usual routine.
  • Night wakings: keep them dark, quiet and boring.
  • Short trips: for a small time difference or a few days, staying near home time can be easier.

More detail is in keeping your baby sleeping while traveling.

Packing safety

If an adult in the family takes melatonin, pack it in a locked or zipped compartment and never leave it on a hotel nightstand. Call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 if a child swallows any.

What to ask the pediatrician

  • “Is there any reason my child should not fly right now, such as an ear infection?”
  • “Is melatonin appropriate for my child at all? If so, which product and timing?”
  • “Which medicines should I pack for illness on the trip?”

What actually helps sleep instead

A child who falls asleep on their own at home brings that skill on vacation. The same routine, sleep sack and white noise travel well. When you get home, go straight back to the usual schedule.

Frequently asked

Can I give my 1 year old melatonin for a long flight?

Only if your pediatrician recommends it. The AAP says melatonin should only be used after a discussion with your pediatrician, and product strength varies widely. Feeding, holding and daylight on arrival are the usual tools.

How long does jet lag last in a toddler?

CDC travel guidance gives an average adjustment of about 1 to 1.5 hours a day, faster going west than east. Morning daylight and local mealtimes help the clock shift. The CDC notes jet lag disturbs children’s sleep just as it does adults’.

Does melatonin help with jet lag?

NIH notes it may help with jet lag, but its long-term safety has not been established. In young children the AAP and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine advise talking with a health care professional before giving it.

What should I do if my toddler eats melatonin gummies?

Call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222. Call 911 if your child collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing or cannot be woken. CDC reported a 530% rise in child melatonin ingestions from 2012 to 2021.
