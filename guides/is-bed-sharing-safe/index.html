Is bed-sharing with my baby safe?

The AAP does not support bed-sharing with a baby under any circumstances, including twins. Risk is highest for babies under 4 months, on sofas and armchairs, and when an adult has been drinking, smokes, or takes sedating medication. Room share instead: your baby sleeps on their back in their own crib or bassinet in your room, for at least the first 6 months.

What the AAP says

The AAP’s 2022 safe-sleep recommendations say it “cannot support bed-sharing under any circumstances.” The AAP’s parent site adds that this includes twins and other multiples. It recommends room sharing without bed sharing for at least the first 6 months instead.

About 3,500 babies die from sleep-related causes in the United States each year, according to the AAP.

When the risk is highest

The AAP’s parent site gives these figures:

  • Bed-sharing risk is 5 to 10 times higher when a baby is younger than 4 months.
  • Risk is up to 67 times higher when a baby sleeps with someone on a sofa, soft armchair or cushion.
  • Risk is more than 10 times higher when a baby bed shares with someone who is very tired, has taken medications, or has used alcohol or drugs, and it is especially high with an adult who smokes or vapes.

The NIH Safe to Sleep campaign groups the situations like this:

Risk levelSituation
Very highA soft surface such as a waterbed, old mattress, sofa or armchair. An adult who is very tired, on drowsy-making medication, or using alcohol or other substances. An adult who smokes or uses tobacco, even outside the bed.
HighA baby younger than 4 months. A sleeping adult who is not the parent, such as a grandparent or a sibling.
Higher than normalA baby born preterm (before 37 weeks) or at low birth weight. Pillows or blankets in the sleep area.

Safe to Sleep also notes that the longer an adult shares a bed with a baby, the higher the baby’s risk. That matters for parents who plan to bed share only for the early hours: the guidance applies to those hours too, and to naps.

Why families end up bed sharing

Usually it is exhaustion, not a choice. A baby who wakes every hour and only settles next to a parent makes the bed feel like the only way anyone sleeps. If you might fall asleep while feeding

This is the situation most parents actually face at 3 a.m. Plan for it instead of hoping it won’t happen.

  • Never feed on a sofa or armchair if you are drowsy. These are the most dangerous places to fall asleep with a baby.
  • If you feed in bed, clear pillows, blankets and soft items away from your side of the bed first, as Safe to Sleep advises.
  • If you fall asleep, put your baby back in their own sleep space as soon as you wake.
  • Set yourself up to stay awake: a light on low, sitting up, a phone timer, or a partner who takes the baby back to the bassinet.

Frequently asked

Is bed-sharing safe if I am breastfeeding and don’t smoke or drink?

The AAP does not support bed-sharing under any circumstances, and babies under 4 months are at high risk regardless of smoking or the sleep surface. Feed in bed if you need to, then return your baby to their own space.

Is a sidecar or in-bed sleeper a safe compromise?

In-bed sleepers are among the products the AAP expected the Safe Sleep for Babies Act to remove from the market. A bassinet or bedside sleeper that meets federal requirements, next to your bed, is the safer setup.

Can my toddler sleep in our bed with the baby?

No. Safe to Sleep lists a sibling or other non-parent sharing the sleep surface as a high-risk situation. Your baby needs their own crib, bassinet or play yard, even if your toddler comes into your bed.

Is falling asleep with my baby on the sofa safer than in bed?

No. It is the most dangerous option. The AAP says the risk is up to 67 times higher on a sofa, soft armchair or cushion. If you feel sleepy, move your baby to their crib first.
