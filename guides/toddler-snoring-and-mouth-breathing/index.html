My toddler snores and breathes through their mouth at night. When should I see the pediatrician?

Occasional snoring with a cold is common. What the AAP guideline says

The AAP’s clinical practice guideline on childhood obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) says pediatricians should ask whether a child snores as part of routine health visits. It notes that snoring is common in children and that almost all children with OSA snore. Based on the stronger studies it reviewed, OSA affects about 1.2% to 5.7% of children.

So most snoring children don’t have sleep apnea. But regular snoring is the signal that should start the conversation.

Signs to mention to your pediatrician

The guideline lists these among the things to ask about:

  • Frequent snoring, three or more nights a week
  • Labored breathing during sleep
  • Gasps, or pauses in breathing that you can see
  • Bedwetting
  • Sleeping in a seated position
  • A bluish color during sleep
  • Morning headaches
  • Daytime sleepiness
  • Attention, learning or behavior problems

The AAP’s parent site also lists sleepiness during the day, difficulty paying attention and behavior problems as signs.

What to bring to the appointment

  • A short video of your child sleeping on a typical night, with sound, especially if you see pauses or gasping.
  • A one- to two-week sleep diary: bedtime, wake time, naps, snoring nights, night waking, bedwetting.
  • Daytime notes: mood, attention, how hard mornings are, whether your child falls asleep in the car more than expected.

Breathing comes first: no sleep plan fixes it. Once your pediatrician has checked it, the sleep habits that built up around broken nights are the part Actually Sleeping fixes.

How it is diagnosed and treated

The guideline recommends an overnight sleep study, called polysomnography, for a snoring child with signs of OSA, or referral to a sleep specialist or ear, nose and throat specialist. During the study, sensors track breathing, oxygen levels and brain waves while your child sleeps.

If OSA is found and the tonsils and adenoids are enlarged, the guideline recommends removing them as the first-line treatment. The AAP’s parent site describes this as the most common and effective treatment, and notes follow-up matters because surgery is not always successful. If surgery doesn’t help or isn’t advised, a CPAP machine, which delivers steady air pressure through a mask, may be recommended.

Babies under 1 year

The AAP guideline specifically excludes infants younger than 1 year. That doesn’t mean snoring in a baby is unimportant: it means babies need individual assessment. Tell your pediatrician about regular snoring, noisy breathing, pauses, or struggling to breathe in a baby. Do not prop up the mattress or use positioners to ease breathing; the AAP says inclined sleep surfaces aren’t safe. See inclined sleepers and positioners.

Snoring during a cold

Snoring that starts with a cold and stops when it clears is usually congestion. The AAP suggests a cool-mist humidifier and saline drops for a stuffy nose; see humidifiers and stuffy noses. If snoring continues after the cold, mention it.

Why it matters for sleep

A child whose breathing is disrupted at night can still be tired the next day: the AAP lists daytime sleepiness as a sign. Sleep apnea is also one of the triggers the American Academy of Sleep Medicine lists for sleepwalking and sleep terrors. No behavioral sleep plan fixes a breathing problem. If your child snores regularly and sleeps poorly, rule out OSA with your pediatrician before working on sleep habits.

Frequently asked

Is it normal for a toddler to snore?

Occasional snoring, especially with a cold, is common. The AAP guideline says snoring is common in children and asks pediatricians to check for it at routine visits. Regular snoring, three or more nights a week, is worth raising with your pediatrician.

Does mouth breathing at night mean sleep apnea?

Not on its own. A blocked nose from a cold can cause it too. Mention it to your pediatrician alongside snoring, pauses or gasps, restless sleep, bedwetting, or daytime sleepiness, which together may point to sleep apnea.

How is sleep apnea in children treated?

The AAP guideline recommends removing enlarged tonsils and adenoids as first-line treatment once a sleep study confirms OSA. CPAP may be used if surgery doesn’t help or isn’t advised. Your pediatrician and specialists decide.

My baby snores. Does the same guideline apply?

No. The AAP guideline excludes infants under 1 year, so babies need individual assessment. Tell your pediatrician about regular snoring, noisy breathing or pauses. Never prop up the mattress or use a positioner.
