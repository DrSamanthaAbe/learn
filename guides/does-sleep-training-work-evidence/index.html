Does sleep training work? What the research says

Yes. A 2006 review of 52 studies found that 94 percent reported behavioral sleep interventions were effective, and more than 80 percent of treated children improved, with gains lasting 3 to 6 months. Randomized trials also show lower rates of maternal depression symptoms, and a five-year follow-up found no lasting harm to children’s emotional health, stress regulation or relationship with their parents. The studies are modest in size and mostly involve babies of 6 months and older.

The key studies

StudyDesignMain findings
Mindell et al., 2006 (Sleep)Review of 52 treatment studies for the American Academy of Sleep Medicine94% of studies reported that the intervention worked; over 80% of treated children showed clinically significant improvement, maintained for 3–6 months. Strongest evidence for unmodified extinction and for preventive parent education; support also for graduated extinction, bedtime fading with positive routines, and scheduled awakenings
Hiscock et al., 2007 (Arch Dis Child)Cluster randomized trial in Melbourne, Australia; 328 mothers who reported an infant sleep problem at 7 months; nurses taught behavioral strategies in structured consultationsFewer sleep problems at 10 months (56% vs 68%) and 12 months (39% vs 55%), and fewer maternal depression symptoms, than usual care
Hiscock et al., 2008 (Pediatrics)Follow-up of the same trial at age 2Mothers in the intervention group were less likely to report clinical depression symptoms (15.4% vs 26.4%); no evidence of adverse effects on children’s mental health or on parenting. Sleep problems: 27.3% vs 32.6%
Price et al., 2012 (Pediatrics)Five-year follow-up of the same trial; 225 of 326 families (69%) assessed at age 6No differences, positive or negative, in emotional and behavioral health, sleep, stress regulation, parent–child closeness or maternal mental health
Gradisar et al., 2016 (Pediatrics)Randomized trial of 43 infants aged 6–16 months: graduated extinction, bedtime fading or sleep education onlyBoth methods shortened the time to fall asleep; graduated extinction also reduced night waking. Cortisol showed small-to-moderate declines in both method groups compared with controls. No differences in attachment or in emotional and behavioral problems 12 months later

What the evidence supports

  • It works for most families. Babies fall asleep faster and wake their parents less.
  • Parents benefit. Improved maternal mood is among the most consistent findings.
  • No lasting harm has been found in the studies that looked, up to age 6.
  • More than one method works. Check-based, parent-present and fading approaches all have support.

What the evidence does not show

  • It is not permanent. By age 6, sleep in the trained and untrained groups looked the same. Sleep training brings relief months or years sooner; it does not create a lifelong advantage, and some children need a refresher after illness, travel or a regression.
  • It does not work for everyone. A minority of families in every study did not improve.
  • Babies still wake. In a 2015 Canadian trial of 235 families, movement sensors showed nearly all babies in both groups still woke 2 or more times a night 6 weeks later, while parents in the intervention group reported far fewer severe sleep problems. The change is largely in how babies resettle and how much they need a parent.
  • Evidence under 6 months is thin. Most trial participants were 6 months or older.
  • Methods without trials. Pick-up-put-down and many branded approaches have not been tested directly.

The criticisms, fairly stated

  • Small samples and parent report. The 2016 trial had 43 babies, and many outcomes rely on parents’ diaries.
  • Loss to follow-up. The five-year study assessed 225 of the 326 families in that analysis (69%).
  • Mixed methods. Families in the Australian trial were taught behavioral techniques over 1 to 3 nurse consultations, so results reflect being offered sleep help, not one specific method.
  • Stress during the process. One small study of 25 babies aged 4 to 10 months in a 5-day inpatient sleep program reported that on the third day infants no longer showed distress at bedtime but their cortisol was raised. It had no control group and drew published criticism; the randomized trial that measured cortisol found small declines, not rises. This question is not fully closed.
  • Absence of evidence. “No harm found” in studies of a few hundred children is reassuring but cannot rule out small effects or effects in particular children.

What this means for your decision

The research supports sleep training as an effective and, as far as has been measured, safe option for healthy babies from about 6 months. It does not say that you must do it. Families who are coping well with night waking are not harming their baby by waiting. The choice turns on how sleep loss is affecting your family. Whatever you choose, safe sleep rules apply every night, and your pediatrician is the authority on your child’s health.

Frequently asked

Is sleep training harmful to babies?

Studies have not found harm. A randomized trial with five-year follow-up found no differences in emotional health, behavior, stress regulation or parent–child closeness at age 6, and a 2016 trial found no differences in attachment a year later, with cortisol slightly lower, not higher, in trained babies.

Does sleep training raise cortisol?

The randomized trial that measured cortisol found small-to-moderate declines in sleep-trained babies compared with controls. One small uncontrolled study reported raised cortisol during an inpatient program; it drew published criticism and its finding has not been confirmed in a controlled trial.

Do the effects of sleep training last?

A review found improvements maintained for 3 to 6 months, and one trial found fewer sleep problems at 10 and 12 months. By age 2 the gap had narrowed, and by age 6 sleep in both groups looked the same, so the benefit is earlier relief for the family, not a permanent difference.

Does the AAP recommend sleep training?

The AAP’s safe sleep policy does not address sleep training methods, and its rules apply whatever you choose. Its parent site suggests that from about 4 months you put babies down before they are asleep so they learn to fall asleep on their own. Ask your own pediatrician about timing.
