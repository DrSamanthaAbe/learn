What is the Ferber method, and does it work?

The Ferber method, also called graduated extinction or timed checks, means putting your baby down awake, leaving the room, and returning for brief checks at gradually longer intervals until they fall asleep. It has randomized-trial support, mostly in babies 6 months and older, and nights often improve within a week. It is not cry it out, because you keep checking.

How it works

  1. Finish a calm bedtime routine with the feed at the start, not the end.
  2. Put your baby in the crib awake, say a short goodnight phrase, and leave.
  3. If your baby cries, wait a set interval, then go in for 1 to 2 minutes. Use a calm voice and perhaps a brief pat. Do not pick up, feed or stay until asleep.
  4. Leave again while your baby is still awake and wait a longer interval.
  5. Repeat until your baby is asleep. Do the same at night wakings, apart from planned feeds.
  6. Lengthen the intervals each night.

What to expect

A common course in practice, not a research finding:

  • Night 1: commonly 30 to 60 minutes of protest crying, sometimes more.
  • Nights 2–3: shorter, though one of these nights can be worse than the first.
  • Nights 4–7: most babies settle within 10 to 15 minutes or less.
  • Naps: slower, usually 1 to 2 weeks.

Does it work? What the research says

Yes, for most families who apply it consistently. A 2006 review of 52 studies for the American Academy of Sleep Medicine found support for graduated extinction; its strongest evidence was for unmodified extinction and preventive parent education. In a 2016 randomized trial of 43 babies aged 6 to 16 months, the graduated extinction group fell asleep faster and woke less than the control group. Their cortisol (a stress hormone) showed small-to-moderate declines compared with controls, and there were no differences in attachment or in emotional and behavioral problems 12 months later. The full picture, including the limits of these studies, is in what the research says.

Who it suits, and who it does not

  • Suits: healthy babies from about 5 to 6 months through the toddler years in a crib; parents who want a clear script and a fast result.
  • Less suited: babies who become more upset each time a parent appears and leaves (shorter, less frequent checks or a different method may fit better); parents who know they will end up picking the baby up after a long cry; babies under 4 months; any baby who is ill or whose night-feed needs are unclear.

Common mistakes

  • Checks that turn into 10 minutes of soothing, or rocking to sleep “just this once.”
  • Feeding at a waking that was not a planned feed.
  • Using it at bedtime but not at 2 a.m.
  • Stopping on night 2 or 3, which is often the hardest point and just before improvement.

The method is a general script. It does not tell you whether your baby’s schedule, feeds or room are working against you; those matter as much as the intervals. See the step-by-step guide for the preparation that makes it work.

Frequently asked

What age can you start the Ferber method?

Many families start around 5 to 6 months, when night-feed needs have dropped for many babies. The trial evidence comes mostly from babies 6 months and older, and some families start from 4 months. Ask your pediatrician about your baby’s feeds first.

Is the Ferber method the same as cry it out?

No. In Ferber you return for brief, timed checks. In cry it out, or unmodified extinction, you do not return until morning apart from planned feeds and safety needs.

How long does the Ferber method take?

As a rule of thumb from practice, often 3 to 7 nights for bedtime and night wakings when applied consistently, and 1 to 2 weeks for naps.

What if the checks make my baby cry harder?

That happens with some babies, often older ones. Options are to make checks shorter and less frequent, or to use a different method. Do not switch back and forth night to night.
