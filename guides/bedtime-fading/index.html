What is bedtime fading?

Bedtime fading means temporarily moving bedtime later, to the time your child actually falls asleep, so they fall asleep quickly, and then shifting it earlier by about 15 minutes every 2 to 3 nights until you reach the bedtime you want. It usually takes 1 to 3 weeks and involves little crying. It is best for babies from about 6 months and toddlers who take 45 minutes or more to fall asleep, and it has randomized-trial support.

Why it works

Sleep pressure builds the longer a child is awake. If bedtime is earlier than the child’s body is ready for, they lie awake, protest, and learn that bed is a place for struggling. Fading lines bedtime up with real sleepiness, so the child falls asleep fast and bed becomes linked with sleep again. Then you move the body clock earlier in small steps.

How to do it

  1. Track for 3 to 5 nights. Note the time your child actually falls asleep, not the time you put them down.
  2. Set a temporary bedtime at the average sleep-onset time, or 15 minutes after it. If your 9-month-old goes down at 7:00 and falls asleep around 8:15, the temporary bedtime is 8:15 to 8:30.
  3. Move the routine to match, starting 20 to 30 minutes before the new bedtime. Keep the time before it calm and dim, not in the crib.
  4. Fix the morning wake time. Wake your child at the same time every day, even after a late night. This is what makes the method work.
  5. Keep naps normal. Do not let extra daytime sleep replace the lost night sleep, and do not cut naps either.
  6. Shift earlier. Once your child falls asleep within about 15 minutes for 2 to 3 nights in a row, move bedtime 15 minutes earlier. Repeat until you reach your target.
  7. If a step fails, go back to the last bedtime that worked for 2 more nights.

What the research says

Bedtime fading was one of two methods tested in a 2016 randomized controlled trial of 43 infants aged 6 to 16 months. Babies in the fading group fell asleep faster than the control group, and there were no differences in cortisol measures or in attachment 12 months later. The earlier American Academy of Sleep Medicine review also lists faded bedtime among the behavioral treatments with supporting evidence.

What it does and does not fix

  • It fixes: long bedtime battles, a bedtime set too early for the child’s body clock, and bed that has become linked with being awake.
  • It does not fix by itself: needing to be fed or rocked to sleep. If your baby is still helped to sleep at the later bedtime, night wakings will continue. Pair fading with putting down awake, or with another method.
  • It is not a fix for early morning waking; a later bedtime rarely produces a later morning.

Cautions

Do not leave the late bedtime in place for weeks. Total sleep still matters: babies of 4 to 12 months need about 12 to 16 hours in 24, and toddlers of 1 to 2 years about 11 to 14. If your child becomes clearly overtired, with more night waking or earlier mornings, move bedtime earlier faster. Fading suits babies from about 6 months; younger babies do not yet have a steady enough body clock for it.

Frequently asked

How long does bedtime fading take?

Usually 1 to 3 weeks: a few nights of tracking, a few nights at the later bedtime, then 15-minute shifts earlier every 2 to 3 nights.

Is bedtime fading a no-cry method?

It is the lowest-crying method that has been studied, because the child is put down when truly sleepy. Some protest is still possible, especially if the child is used to being helped to sleep.

Does bedtime fading help with night wakings?

Only partly. In the 2016 trial it shortened the time to fall asleep more clearly than it reduced night wakings. Night wakings improve most when the child also learns to fall asleep without help.

Can I use bedtime fading with a toddler?

Yes. It works well for toddlers who stall, call out or leave the room for an hour at bedtime. Combine it with a consistent routine and a clear response to getting out of bed.
