How do I sleep train my baby? A step-by-step guide

Sleep training means teaching a baby to fall asleep in the crib without being fed, rocked or held to sleep, so they can link sleep cycles on their own overnight. Many families start between 4 and 6 months. Spend a week fixing schedule, routine and room, then hold one method consistently for 2 weeks. Nights often improve within a week; naps take longer.

Your pediatrician is the authority on your child’s health. Confirm growth, night-feed needs and any medical concerns with them before changing how your baby sleeps.

Step 1: Check readiness

Start when every line below is true. If one is not, fix that first.

  • Age: at least 4 months (corrected age for babies born early). The AAP’s parent site notes that babies do not have regular sleep cycles until about 4 months, and from that age suggests putting babies down before they are asleep so they learn to fall asleep on their own. Before that, shape habits gently rather than train; see the best age to sleep train.
  • Health: growing well, no current illness, and your pediatrician has told you how many night feeds your baby still needs.
  • Safe sleep space: on the back, on a firm, flat mattress in an empty crib or bassinet. No inclines, wedges, positioners, bumpers or loose blankets. Swaddle off once there are signs of rolling.
  • Calendar: 2 weeks with no travel, house move, new childcare or visitors sleeping in the baby’s room.
  • Adults: everyone who does bedtime agrees on the plan and will respond the same way at 2 a.m.

Step 2: Prep week

Most failed attempts skip this. Spend 5 to 7 days on the foundations before you change how your baby falls asleep.

  1. Log 3 days. Write down wake time, naps, feeds, bedtime and every night waking. The pattern usually shows the cause.
  2. Fix the schedule. Set a wake time within the same 30 minutes daily and age-appropriate wake windows. A baby who is under-tired or badly overtired will protest any method.
  3. Move the feed. Put the last feed at the start of the bedtime routine, with lights on, so your baby finishes it awake. This loosens the feed-to-sleep association before night one.
  4. Build a 20–30 minute routine. Same steps, same order: feed, bath or wash, diaper, sleep sack, book, song, crib.
  5. Set the room. Dark enough that you cannot read, continuous white noise at a moderate volume placed away from the crib, comfortably cool.
  6. Decide night feeds in advance. Write down which feeds stay, roughly when, and how you will respond to every other waking.

Step 3: Choose a method

All of these teach the same skill. They differ in how much you are present and how fast they work. The method you can carry out consistently is the right one.

Step 4: Night by night

This is a common pattern, not a research finding. Your baby may move faster or slower.

  • Night 1. Routine, then into the crib fully awake. Expect the longest protest of the process, commonly 30 to 60 minutes and sometimes longer. Respond exactly as your method says. Treat night wakings the same way, apart from planned feeds.
  • Night 2. Often similar or a little shorter. Occasionally worse than night 1.
  • Night 3. Usually a clear drop in time to fall asleep. Some babies have a late surge here (an “extinction burst”) just before things settle.
  • Nights 4–7. Most babies fall asleep within 5 to 15 minutes with little or no crying, and night wakings shrink to the planned feeds.
  • Week 2. Consolidation. Early mornings and naps are still improving. A brief relapse around days 10–14 is common and passes if your response stays the same.

Step 5: Naps

Daytime sleep pressure is lower, so naps lag behind nights. Many families start with bedtime and add naps on day 2 or 3. Use the same method, give each nap attempt up to about an hour in the crib, and if no sleep happens, get the baby up and try again at the next window. Keep one consistent approach for at least a week before judging it. Thirty-minute naps are usually the last thing to lengthen.

Common mistakes

  • Drowsy instead of awake. A baby put down nearly asleep has not practiced falling asleep.
  • Inconsistent responses. Holding out for 40 minutes and then rocking to sleep teaches a baby to cry for 40 minutes.
  • Switching methods every night or two. Give one method 7 nights.
  • A bedtime that is too late. Overtired babies fight sleep harder and wake earlier.
  • Bedtime only. Training bedtime but feeding or rocking back to sleep at every waking keeps the old habit alive.
  • Starting during illness or travel. Pause, then restart cleanly.

When to get help

Stop and call your pediatrician if your baby snores or gasps in sleep, seems to be in pain, is feeding poorly, or is not gaining weight. If your baby is healthy and you have been fully consistent for 7 to 10 nights without clear progress, the plan needs changing, not more effort; see what to do when sleep training is not working. Frequently asked

What is the easiest sleep training method?

There is no single easiest method. Timed checks are the simplest to follow because the script is clear; the chair method feels easier emotionally for many parents but takes longer and demands more patience. The easiest one is the one both caregivers can repeat the same way every night.

How long does it take to sleep train a baby?

As a rule of thumb from practice, nights often improve within about a week with check-based methods and over 1 to 3 weeks with gradual, parent-present methods. Naps usually take longer. Plan on 2 weeks of consistency.

Do I have to stop night feeds to sleep train?

No. Sleep training teaches falling asleep independently; night weaning is a separate decision. Keep the feeds your pediatrician says your baby needs and apply your method to the other wakings.

Can I sleep train without letting my baby cry?

You can choose methods where you stay with your baby and respond throughout, which usually means less intense crying. No method can promise zero protest, because any change to how a baby falls asleep is frustrating at first.
