Are natural or homeopathic baby sleep drops and teething tablets safe?

Not reliably. The FDA has approved no homeopathic product and has not reviewed them for safety or effectiveness. FDA testing of homeopathic teething tablets found belladonna levels that sometimes far exceeded the label, a risk the agency called unnecessary for children under 2. Herbal “sleep” products have weak evidence. Check with your child’s pediatrician before using any of them.

The short answer

“Natural” and “homeopathic” are marketing and regulatory categories. They do not mean tested, gentle or safe for babies. Some of these products contain plant extracts that are poisonous in larger amounts, and manufacturing control has been a documented problem.

What the FDA says about homeopathic products

  • No approvals. The FDA states that there are no FDA-approved products labeled as homeopathic, and that homeopathic products have not been reviewed by the FDA for safety and effectiveness.
  • Risky ingredients. The FDA warns that homeopathic makers use sources that include known poisons or toxins, and that poor manufacturing controls can produce products that exceed their labeled strength. Some are marketed for infants and children.
  • Teething tablets. FDA lab analysis of homeopathic teething tablets found belladonna alkaloids in amounts that varied from tablet to tablet and, in some, far exceeded the label. The FDA said this poses an unnecessary risk to infants and children under 2. The FDA also lists homeopathic teething tablets among teething products associated with serious risks.
  • Cough and cold. The FDA advises against giving homeopathic cough and cold medicine to children younger than 4.

Herbal and supplement “sleep drops”

  • Chamomile: NIH says there is no conclusive evidence from clinical trials on whether it helps sleep. People allergic to ragweed or related plants can react to it.
  • Valerian: clinical trials have had inconsistent results, and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine’s 2017 guideline recommended against valerian for chronic insomnia in adults.
  • Labels you cannot fully trust: the AAP notes that some sleep supplements have been found to contain other substances, such as CBD or serotonin. Read every ingredient. See is melatonin safe for babies.
  • Supplements in general: NIH notes that many have not been tested in children, may contain contaminants, and sometimes do not match their labels.

Safer ways to ease teething

The FDA suggests gently rubbing your baby’s gums with a clean finger or offering a firm rubber teether (not liquid-filled, and not frozen), with supervision. It warns against benzocaine products for teething and against teething jewelry such as amber necklaces, which have been linked to strangulation and choking. For pain medicine, see does teething medicine help a baby sleep, and ask your pediatrician.

What to ask the pediatrician

  • “Is this product safe at my baby’s age, and is there any reason to use it?”
  • “Could my baby’s waking have a medical cause we should look at?”
  • “Which pain reliever, if any, is appropriate for my baby’s age and weight?”

What actually helps sleep instead

  • Predictability: the same bedtime window and the same short routine each night.
  • Environment: dark room, steady white noise if you use it, and a safe, empty crib.
  • How your baby falls asleep: babies who practice falling asleep in the crib are better at resettling between sleep cycles. No drop or tablet teaches that skill.
  • Short-lived events stay short: comfort a teething or unwell baby fully, then return to the usual routine once they are well.

Frequently asked

Are homeopathic teething tablets FDA approved?

No. The FDA states that no homeopathic products are FDA-approved and that they have not been reviewed for safety and effectiveness. FDA testing of teething tablets found belladonna levels that sometimes far exceeded the label.

Is chamomile tea okay for a toddler’s sleep?

Ask your pediatrician first. NIH says there is no conclusive clinical-trial evidence that chamomile helps sleep, and people allergic to ragweed or related plants can react to it. Never sweeten anything with honey for a baby under 12 months.

Are natural sleep drops safer than medicine?

Not necessarily. NIH notes that “natural” does not necessarily mean safe, that many supplements have not been tested in children, and that some contain contaminants or do not match their labels.

What should I use for teething at night instead?

The FDA suggests rubbing the gums with a clean finger or a firm rubber teether, not frozen. Ask your pediatrician whether a pain reliever is appropriate for your baby’s age and weight, and use the label directions.
