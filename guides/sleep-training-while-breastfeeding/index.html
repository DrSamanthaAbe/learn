Can I sleep train and keep breastfeeding?

Yes. Sleep training changes how your baby falls asleep, not whether they are breastfed, and you do not have to night wean to do it. Most families move the bedtime feed to the start of the routine, keep the night feeds the pediatrician recommends, and use their chosen method for the other wakings. Ideally breastfeeding is well established and your baby is at least 4 months old and growing well.

A baby can fall asleep independently and still have one or two night feeds. Many breastfed babies do exactly that for months.

How to do both

  1. Feed at the start of the bedtime routine, in a lit room, and keep your baby awake through it. Then bath, sleep sack, book, song, crib.
  2. Offer full feeds in the day, about every 2.5 to 3.5 hours, in a calm spot if your baby is distractible.
  3. Decide the night feeds with your pediatrician. A common rule is to feed if it has been at least 3 to 4 hours since the last feed, and to use your method for wakings before that.
  4. Keep night feeds plain. Dim light, a full feed, then back into the crib awake or lightly drowsy. Feeding fully to sleep now and then at 3 a.m. is not a disaster; doing it at bedtime is what maintains the feed-to-sleep association.
  5. Use one method at bedtime and non-feed wakings. See the step-by-step guide.

Protecting your milk supply

  • Reduce feeds gradually. If you are dropping a night feed, shorten it by a few minutes every night or two, or stretch the time before it, so your supply adjusts slowly.
  • Do not stay painfully full. If you wake engorged, express just enough for comfort, and call your doctor or a lactation consultant if you develop a painful lump, redness or fever.
  • Expect calories to shift to the day. Babies usually feed more in the morning and daytime when night feeds fall. Offer an extra daytime feed during the change.
  • Watch diapers and weight. If wet diapers drop or you are worried about intake, pause and call your pediatrician or a lactation consultant.
  • If you pump at work or have had supply concerns, involve a lactation consultant before removing night feeds.

Does sleep training reduce breastfeeding?

The evidence is limited. The randomized trials of sleep interventions focused on sleep and maternal mood, and their long-term follow-up found no harms, but they were not designed to measure breastfeeding duration. One observational study found that babies who slept 6 to 8 hours straight were much less likely to be breastfed; that shows an association, not that one causes the other. Keep an eye on your own supply. The AAP notes that human milk reduces the risk of SIDS, which is a good reason to protect breastfeeding while you improve sleep.

Who settles the baby?

A breastfed baby often smells milk and protests harder when the nursing parent responds. For the first few nights it can help if the other caregiver handles non-feed wakings. If you are on your own, a consistent response still works; it may take a night or two longer.

When to wait

Hold off if your baby is under 4 months, has slow weight gain, has latch or supply problems that are still being worked on, or is ill. Frequently asked

Do I have to night wean to sleep train?

No. You can keep one or more night feeds and still teach your baby to fall asleep independently at bedtime and at other wakings.

Will sleep training hurt my milk supply?

Not if night feeds are reduced gradually and daytime feeds increase to match. Supply follows demand, so drop feeds slowly and express for comfort if you become engorged.

How do I know if my baby is hungry or just waking from habit?

Clues for hunger: a long gap since the last feed, a full active feed, and going straight back to sleep. Clues for habit: waking every 1 to 2 hours, sucking briefly and dozing off. Your pediatrician can tell you how many feeds are needed at your baby’s age and weight.

Can I still nurse my baby to sleep for naps?

It is your choice, but mixed messages slow progress. Most babies learn faster when they fall asleep the same way at naps and at night, so try to feed after naps instead of before them.
