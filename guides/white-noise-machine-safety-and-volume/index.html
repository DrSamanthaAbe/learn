Is white noise safe for my baby, and how loud should it be?

White noise can be used safely if it stays quiet and away from the crib. A 2014 study in Pediatrics tested 14 infant sleep machines at maximum volume: all were louder than the 50-decibel limit recommended for hospital nurseries when measured 30 cm away, and three went above 85 decibels. Use the lowest volume that works and place the machine across the room.

What the research found

Researchers measured 14 infant sleep machines played at maximum volume, at 30, 100 and 200 cm from the machine, and adjusted for the size of a 6-month-old’s ear canal. Their findings, published in Pediatrics in 2014:

  • At 30 cm, every machine was louder than 50 A-weighted decibels, the recommended noise limit for infants in hospital nurseries.
  • Three machines produced more than 85 decibels, which exceeds occupational limits for accumulated noise exposure in adults.
  • The authors concluded that these machines can produce sound levels that may damage infant hearing, and that the loudest ones played for more than 8 hours could put hearing at risk.

The study measured machines at their loudest. The practical message is about volume and distance, not about avoiding sound altogether.

How to use white noise safely

  1. Distance. Place the machine across the room, not on the crib rail or inside the crib. Sound drops quickly with distance.
  2. Volume. Use the lowest setting that masks household noise. If you have to raise your voice to talk over it next to the crib, it is too loud.
  3. Check it. A free sound-meter app on your phone, held where your baby’s head lies, gives a rough reading. Aim well below 50 decibels.
  4. Cords. Keep the cord out of reach. The CPSC warns never to place corded baby cameras within three feet of a crib because of strangulation risk; treat any corded device the same way.
  5. Nothing in the crib. Plush toys with built-in sound machines add a soft object to the sleep space. Keep the crib bare and the sound source on a shelf.

Why parents use it

Steady sound can mask the noises that wake a light sleeper: a dog, a sibling, traffic, a door. It is also a portable sleep cue: the same sound at home, at a grandparent’s house, or in a hotel tells your baby it is time to sleep. Many families find it especially helpful when siblings share a room or when naps happen during a noisy part of the day.

Continuous or timer

Either can work. A sound that switches off mid-night can wake some babies at the very moment you hoped they would link sleep cycles. If you run it all night, keep the volume low and the machine at a distance.

Choosing a sound

  • A steady, even sound such as fan or rain noise is the usual choice.
  • Avoid sounds with melodies, voices, or sudden changes, which can catch attention instead of fading into the background.
  • Avoid machines with bright lights or projections. A dark room supports sleep; see blackout curtains and night lights.

Will my baby become dependent on it

Some children do get used to it, the same way some adults sleep with a fan. That is rarely a problem. If you want to stop later, turn the volume down a little every few nights until it is off. Many families keep it into the toddler years without trouble.

White noise is a helper, not a fix. If your baby wakes every hour despite it, the cause is usually how they fall asleep at bedtime. See how to get a baby to sleep through the night.

Frequently asked

How loud should white noise be for a baby?

Use the lowest volume that masks household sounds, with the machine across the room. A 2014 study found machines at maximum volume exceeded the 50-decibel hospital nursery limit at 30 cm, so keep it well below that.

Is it safe to play white noise all night?

Yes, at a low volume and a distance from the crib. The 2014 study raised concern about the loudest machines played for more than 8 hours, which is why distance and volume matter.

Can I put the sound machine in the crib?

No. Keep the crib bare, and keep cords well out of reach. The CPSC warns never to place corded baby cameras within three feet of a crib; the same caution makes sense for any corded device.
