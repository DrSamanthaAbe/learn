My baby rolls onto their tummy in their sleep. Should I flip them back?

Always place your baby down on their back. If they can roll both ways, back to tummy and tummy to back, the AAP says you don’t need to keep turning them back over. If they roll onto their tummy but can’t yet roll back, gently return them to their back. Stop swaddling as soon as your baby shows signs of trying to roll.

The AAP rule

Babies up to 1 year should always be placed on their back to sleep, for naps and at night. What happens after that depends on what your baby can do:

Your babyWhat to do
Rolls back to tummy, cannot roll tummy to backGently turn them onto their back. Practise rolling both ways during awake tummy time.
Rolls comfortably both waysThe AAP says you don’t need to keep turning your baby back over. Keep placing them down on their back.
Is still swaddled and showing signs of rollingStop swaddling now. Arms need to be free to push up and roll back.

When rolling starts

The AAP’s parent site says rolling usually happens around 3 to 4 months, but it can happen earlier. That is also the reason to stop swaddling early: once a baby is trying to roll, a swaddle can stop them using their arms. See when and how to stop swaddling.

Why a bare crib matters more now

A baby who can roll can end up face down near anything in the crib. That is why the rules about a firm, flat mattress and nothing but a fitted sheet matter even more once rolling starts. No pillows, blankets, bumpers, stuffed toys, loungers or positioners. The FDA specifically warns parents never to use sleep positioners to hold a baby on their back or side; they can cause suffocation.

Build the skill during the day

Rolling is a motor skill, and practice helps. The AAP says to always stay with your baby during tummy time and make sure they are awake.

  • Give several short tummy-time sessions a day on the floor.
  • Put a toy just out of reach to one side to encourage rolling.
  • Once your baby rolls back to tummy, help them practise the return: tummy to back.
  • Practise both directions, so they are not stuck on one side.

The 3 a.m. problem

Many babies go through a short stretch where they roll onto their tummy, cannot get back, and cry. You may be up several times a night turning them over. Some ways to make it easier:

  • Go in calmly and quickly. Turn your baby onto their back, use a few words or a pat, and leave.
  • Keep it boring. No picking up and rocking unless your baby is truly upset.
  • Double the daytime practice. The faster they learn to roll back, the faster this stops.
  • Don’t restrain. Do not swaddle, wedge, or use any product meant to stop rolling.

Side sleeping

The side position is not a safe alternative to the back. Place your baby on their back every time. If they roll to their side or tummy on their own and can roll both ways, you can leave them there.

Sleep sacks and rolling

A sleep sack with arms out is the usual next step after the swaddle. It keeps your baby warm without loose bedding and leaves their arms free. Avoid any sleep sack or swaddle that is weighted; the AAP says weighted products are not safe on or near a baby.

Rolling often arrives alongside other changes around 4 months. If sleep has fallen apart at the same time, see the 4 month regression or a real sleep problem.

Frequently asked

Is it dangerous if my baby sleeps on their tummy?

Always place your baby on their back. If they roll to their tummy on their own and can roll both ways, the AAP says you don’t need to turn them back. If they can only roll one way, return them to their back.

Should I stop swaddling the moment my baby rolls?

Sooner. The AAP says stop swaddling when your baby looks like they’re trying to roll. Rolling usually happens around 3 to 4 months, and it can start earlier, so watch for early signs.

Can I use a positioner to stop my baby rolling?

No. The FDA says never use infant sleep positioners because they can cause suffocation and death. Keep the crib bare with only a fitted sheet, and practise rolling both ways during awake tummy time.
