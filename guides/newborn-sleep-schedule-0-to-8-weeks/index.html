What is a realistic newborn sleep schedule (0–8 weeks)?

There is no clock schedule for a newborn, only a rhythm: feed on cue, stay awake briefly (a common rule of thumb is 45–60 minutes, rising toward 60–90 minutes by 6–8 weeks), sleep, repeat. Expect 5 or more naps, sleep spread across day and night, often only 1–2 hours at a time at first, a total the AAP puts at about 16–17 hours, and a late “bedtime”.

What is typical from 0 to 8 weeks

A newborn day is a loop of feeding, a short time awake and sleep, repeated around the clock. The ranges below describe what is common. They are not targets.

What is changing developmentally

A newborn has no settled body clock. Circadian rhythms develop around 2 to 3 months, and by about 3 months melatonin and cortisol follow a day–night cycle, which is why newborn sleep is scattered across 24 hours and why day–night confusion is normal. Newborns fall asleep through active (REM-like) sleep, so grunting, twitching and brief cries during sleep do not always mean the baby is awake. The AAP says fussing usually peaks at about 3 hours a day by 6 weeks and declines by 3 to 4 months.

The AASM consensus on sleep duration deliberately gives no recommendation under 4 months, because normal sleep varies so widely at that age and there is too little evidence linking it to health outcomes. See how infant sleep cycles develop for what comes next.

Why a newborn schedule rarely holds

  • Day–night confusion: with no body clock yet, night is not different from day. It is expected, and it is why clock times mean little in these weeks.
  • Only sleeps when held: very common now. Contact sleep is fine while you are fully awake. It becomes something to plan around later; see contact napping.
  • The evening fussy stretch: the AAP says colicky fussing tends to fall between 6 p.m. and midnight, which is exactly when a printed schedule says “bedtime”.
  • Overtired by evening: how long a newborn can comfortably stay awake varies more than any chart shows; see wake windows by age.

Safe sleep from the first night

  • On the back for every sleep, on a firm, flat surface, in an empty bassinet or crib.
  • Share a room, not a bed, for at least the first 6 months (AAP).
  • If your baby falls asleep in a car seat, swing or carrier, move them to a firm, flat surface on their back as soon as you can.
  • Stop swaddling as soon as your baby shows any sign of trying to roll. The AAP says some babies start working on rolling as early as 2 months.

Is this age right for sleep training?

Not with any method that drops feeds or leaves a newborn to cry; those are not for this age. Foundations can start now: safe sleep, full feeds, and a predictable shape to days and nights. Frequently asked

Should I wake my newborn to feed?

Follow your pediatrician’s guidance. Newborns are often woken to feed until weight gain is established, and only your pediatrician can tell you when longer stretches are fine for your baby. Nothing about sleep should override that decision.

When do newborns start sleeping longer at night?

Circadian rhythms develop around 2 to 3 months, and night sleep starts to consolidate from about 2 months. Many babies then begin to produce one longer stretch, usually the first of the night. The range is wide and depends on feeding and growth.

What time should a newborn go to bed?

Late is normal in the first weeks, often 9 to 11 p.m. as a rough rule of thumb. Bedtime tends to drift earlier as the body clock develops. Where it lands for your baby depends on feeds and naps, which is what a custom plan from Actually Sleeping maps out.

Can a newborn be on a schedule?

Not a clock schedule. A repeating pattern of feed, short awake time and sleep is realistic. Fixed feeding or nap times are not, and feeds should never be delayed to fit a timetable. Actually Sleeping builds foundations from the newborn stage without touching night feeds.
