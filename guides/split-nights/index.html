Why is my baby awake for hours in the middle of the night?

A baby who is wide awake and often cheerful for 1–3 hours in the middle of the night is having a split night. It usually means more time is being offered for sleep than your baby can fill: too much daytime sleep, a bedtime that is too early, or 13–14 hours in the crib for a baby who sleeps 11. The fix is a schedule matched to your baby’s real sleep need.

How to recognize one

  • Awake for an hour or more, typically somewhere between midnight and 4 a.m.
  • Often calm: babbling, rolling around, practicing skills. Crying usually starts only once boredom sets in.
  • Feeding, rocking or holding does not bring sleep back quickly.
  • It happens most nights, at a similar time.

This is different from frequent brief wakings every 1.5–2 hours, which point to a sleep association instead. See waking every 2 hours.

Why it happens

Two systems keep a baby asleep: sleep pressure, which builds while awake and is spent in the first half of the night, and the body clock, which carries the second half. When a baby has slept a lot by day or gone to bed very early, pressure runs out before the clock takes over. The gap is the split.

The arithmetic behind it

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 12–16 hours per 24 for babies 4–12 months and 11–14 hours for ages 1–2, naps included. Individual babies sit at different points in that range, and that is the catch. A schedule that is perfect for a baby who needs 15 hours leaves a baby who needs 13.5 with two hours to spare, and those hours turn up at 2 a.m.

Why sample schedules make it worse

Most published schedules are built for an average baby. Followed closely, they can offer more sleep than your baby needs, and the usual response to a split night (sleeping in, an extra nap, an earlier bedtime “to catch up”) feeds the split. Other triggers pile on top:

  • Nap transitions. Hanging onto a nap your baby has outgrown is a classic cause. See nap transitions.
  • New motor skills. Crawling and standing get rehearsed at 2 a.m.
  • Jet lag or a clock change, which shift the body clock directly.
  • Catch-up sleep after illness, when long recovery naps linger after the illness is over.
  • Night feeds that anchor the waking in an older baby whose pediatrician has said they are optional.

Frequently asked

Is a split night the same as a sleep regression?

No. A regression is a burst of waking around a developmental change. A split night is one long wakeful period caused by more sleep opportunity than the baby needs, and it continues until the schedule changes.

Should I get my baby up and play during a split night?

No. Treating it as daytime tends to teach the body clock to expect it. The lasting fix is the daytime schedule and bedtime, not what you do at 2 a.m., which is why a plan focuses there.

How long does it take to fix split nights?

In practice, often 1–2 weeks once the schedule matches your baby’s actual sleep need. In a Actually Sleeping plan, Dr. Sam adjusts the timings daily from your log so that it gets there without guesswork.

Can an early bedtime cause split nights?

Yes, when it adds up to more time in bed than your baby can sleep. An early bedtime helps an overtired baby and hurts one who is already getting enough.
