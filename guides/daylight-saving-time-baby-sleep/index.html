How do I adjust my baby’s sleep for daylight saving time?

The clock change moves your baby’s schedule by an hour that their body clock has not moved. In spring, bedtime arrives before your baby feels tired; in fall, your baby is tired early and wakes an hour early. Light is the main thing that moves the body clock, and a baby who already falls asleep independently adjusts with far less fuss.

What the clock change does

Your baby’s body clock does not read the wall clock. After the spring change, a 7 p.m. bedtime feels like 6 p.m. to the body, so falling asleep is harder and mornings run later. After the fall change, 7 p.m. feels like 8 p.m., so your baby is tired early and up an hour early. Fall is the one parents dread, because nobody wants a 5 a.m. start.

What moves the body clock

Light is the strongest signal. The CDC notes that morning light shifts the body clock earlier and evening light shifts it later. Meals and activity are timing cues too. That is why the clock change interacts with everything else in your baby’s day: a bright evening in spring pushes the clock later just when you want bedtime earlier, and a dark fall morning does little to stop a 5 a.m. wake-up.

Why some babies struggle more than others

  • Babies who need help to fall asleep. A harder bedtime means more rocking or feeding, and those habits can outlast the clock change.
  • Early risers. In fall, a baby who already wakes at 6 can lock in a 5 a.m. start. See early morning waking.
  • Families with fixed daycare times, where the day cannot shift with the baby.
  • Babies in a nap transition, where the schedule is already unsettled.

Generic shift charts assume a baby who falls asleep easily and has a predictable day. For a baby who does not, the clock change is often just the trigger for a problem that was already there.

What to expect

In practice most babies settle within 3–7 days after a clock change. Naps may be a little off during that week, and bedtimes may take longer. A baby whose sleep was already fragile may take longer, and new habits started during the adjustment week, such as extra rocking or a feed back to sleep at 5 a.m., can stick. If sleep has not settled after a couple of weeks, the clock change is rarely the real cause; it has simply exposed how your baby falls asleep.

Frequently asked

Does a consistent wake time help after the clocks change?

Yes. A consistent morning wake time with bright light is one of the strongest signals for the body clock. Meals and activity at the new times help too.

How long does it take a baby to adjust to daylight saving time?

In practice, usually 3–7 days. Babies who are sensitive to schedule changes, or whose sleep was already fragile, may take up to two weeks.

My baby is waking at 5 a.m. after the fall time change. What do I do?

Fall moves the body clock an hour earlier by the wall clock, and for an early riser that can stick. Early waking has several causes, so a Actually Sleeping plan sets the schedule and the response for your baby rather than a one-size rule.

Is spring or fall harder for babies?

Fall is usually harder because it produces early mornings. Spring mostly causes a few slow bedtimes, because bedtime arrives before the body clock is ready.
