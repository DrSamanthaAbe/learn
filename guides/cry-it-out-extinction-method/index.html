What is cry it out (extinction), and is it safe?

Cry it out, called unmodified extinction in research, means putting a healthy baby down awake after a full bedtime routine and not returning until a planned feed or morning. Studies have not found lasting harm to children’s emotional health, stress regulation or attachment from behavioral sleep training. It often works within a week, is commonly used from about 6 months, and only suits healthy babies in a safe sleep space.

Your pediatrician is the authority on your child’s health. Confirm growth, night-feed needs and any medical concerns with them before changing how your baby sleeps.

What it is, and what it is not

Extinction is a structured method: a loving routine, a fed and dry baby, a safe crib, a set bedtime, planned night feeds if your pediatrician wants them, and a parent who is watching a monitor. It is not ignoring a baby all day, leaving a sick or hungry baby, or closing the door for 12 hours regardless of what happens. You always go in for illness, vomiting, a limb stuck in the crib slats, or anything that sounds wrong.

How it works

  1. Confirm with your pediatrician that your baby is healthy and how many night feeds are needed.
  2. Do the bedtime routine, with the feed first.
  3. Put your baby down awake, say goodnight, leave.
  4. Do not go back in for crying alone. Feed at the planned times, then put down awake again.
  5. Start the day at a set time, at least 10 to 11 hours after bedtime.

Is it safe? What the evidence says

  • A 2006 review of 52 studies for the American Academy of Sleep Medicine found strong support for unmodified extinction, alongside preventive parent education.
  • A five-year follow-up of a randomized trial of behavioral sleep techniques found no differences at age 6 in emotional or behavioral problems, stress regulation, or the parent–child relationship. Families in that trial were taught behavioral techniques by nurses; it was not a test of unmodified extinction.
  • A 2016 randomized trial of graduated extinction found small-to-moderate declines in cortisol compared with controls, and no differences in attachment at 12 months.

The honest limits: samples are modest, most participants were 6 months or older, and pure extinction has less long-term follow-up than check-based methods. The research supports “no lasting harm found,” which is not the same as proof for every child. See what the research says.

What to expect

A common course in practice, not a research finding:

  • Night 1: often 45 to 90 minutes of crying, sometimes longer.
  • Night 2: usually shorter; occasionally longer.
  • Nights 3–5: most babies fall asleep within about 10 minutes.
  • Later: a brief return of crying a week or two in is common and fades if you respond the same way.

Who should not use it

  • Babies under 4 months, and usually not before 6 months.
  • Babies who are ill, in pain, or have unresolved feeding, weight or breathing concerns. See sleep training a baby with reflux.
  • Any baby not in a safe sleep space: back, firm flat mattress, empty crib. See safe sleep and sleep training.
  • Families who are not sure they can see it through. Crying for an hour and then being rocked to sleep is harder on everyone than a method with more parental presence, done consistently.

Alternatives

If extinction is not for you, timed checks, the chair method, pick-up-put-down and bedtime fading all teach the same skill with more parental presence; they are compared in methods compared. For transparency, Actually Sleeping does not use cry it out. Plans are built around the individual baby with daily 1:1 support.

Frequently asked

How long is too long to let a baby cry it out?

There is no research-defined limit. Many families set their own ceiling in advance, and in practice many babies fall asleep within 45 to 90 minutes on the first night. If crying sounds different from protest, or your baby may be ill or unsafe, go in.

Does cry it out cause attachment problems?

Studies that measured attachment and the parent–child relationship after behavioral sleep training, including a five-year follow-up, found no differences from children who were not sleep trained.

Can I do cry it out at 4 months?

Many families wait until about 6 months for extinction, because many 4-month-olds still need night feeds and most of the research involved babies 6 months and older. Gentler shaping and gradual methods fit better at 4 months. Ask your pediatrician.

What if my baby vomits from crying?

Go in, clean up calmly with the lights low, offer comfort briefly, and put your baby back down awake. If it happens more than once, pause and talk to your pediatrician, and consider a method with more parental presence.
