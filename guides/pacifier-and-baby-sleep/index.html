Is the pacifier helping or hurting my baby’s sleep?

It is helping if your baby takes it at bedtime and sleeps; the AAP recommends offering a pacifier at sleep onset because it is associated with a lower risk of SIDS. It is hurting if you are up several times a night replacing it. Once hand control allows, often from about 7 months, many babies learn to replace it themselves; before that, keep replacing it or teach your baby to fall asleep without it.

What the AAP says

  • Offer a pacifier when placing your baby down for naps and at night. The protective association holds even if it falls out after the baby is asleep.
  • You do not need to put it back in once your baby is asleep, and you should not force it if your baby refuses.
  • If you are breastfeeding, wait until feeding is well established before introducing one.
  • Nothing attached: do not hang a pacifier around the neck or attach it to clothing, blankets or stuffed toys during sleep. Those are strangulation, suffocation and choking hazards.

Help or hindrance? A quick test

HelpingHurting
Baby takes it at sleep onset, it falls out, baby stays asleepBaby wakes crying each time it falls out
Zero to one replacement per nightThree or more replacements a night, often every 45–120 minutes
Naps are a reasonable lengthNaps end at 30–40 minutes when the pacifier drops

The second column is a sleep association: your baby fell asleep sucking, surfaced between cycles, and needs the same condition back. It works exactly like feeding to sleep, just faster to fix.

Option 1: keep it and teach your baby to manage it (often from about 7 months)

  1. During the day, practice: put the pacifier in your baby’s hand instead of their mouth and let them bring it up.
  2. At bedtime, offer a plain pacifier with nothing attached. Some families leave a spare within reach in the crib; check with your pediatrician that this suits your baby, and keep everything else out of the sleep space.
  3. At night, guide your baby’s hand to a pacifier rather than inserting it. After a few nights, just point or tap the mattress.
  4. Expect about a week before the night wakings for it stop.

Option 2: fall asleep without it

For a baby under about 7 months who cannot yet replace it, or a family tired of the nightly search, the cleaner route is to stop using it for sleep. Babies typically protest for two to four nights, then settle. Because of the AAP guidance, this is a trade-off worth raising with your pediatrician for a baby under 6 months: most SIDS deaths happen between 1 and 4 months, and more than 90% before 6 months. A middle path: offer it at put-down and do not replace it afterwards, while your baby learns to resettle another way.

Toddlers

Many families phase the pacifier out in the toddler years; ask your pediatrician or dentist about timing for your child. Limit it to the crib first, then pick a day, tell your child plainly, and expect a few rough bedtimes.

Frequently asked

Do I need to put the pacifier back in after my baby falls asleep?

No. The AAP says it does not need to be reinserted once the baby is asleep.

At what age can a baby replace their own pacifier?

In practice many learn from around 7 to 8 months, once they have the hand control to pick it up and turn it around. There is no research-defined age, and daytime practice helps.

Can I use a pacifier attached to a stuffed animal for sleep?

Not for sleep. AAP and NIH safe sleep guidance says not to attach pacifiers to clothing, blankets or stuffed toys during sleep. Use a plain pacifier.

Will taking the pacifier away ruin my baby’s sleep?

Usually only for a few nights. Babies adapt quickly when the change is consistent and they have another way to fall asleep.
