Sleep training isn’t working. What should I do?

First check how long you have been fully consistent: as a rule of thumb, check-based methods need 5 to 7 nights and gradual methods 10 to 14. If you are past that with no clear progress, the cause is nearly always one of five things: inconsistent responses, a baby who is put down drowsy or still fed to sleep, a schedule that does not fit, night feeds without a plan, or a medical issue. Find which one applies and fix it before changing methods.

Your pediatrician is the authority on your child’s health. Confirm growth, night-feed needs and any medical concerns with them before changing how your baby sleeps.

Is it really not working?

Progress is rarely a straight line. These are normal and do not mean failure:

  • Night 2 or 3 worse than night 1.
  • A burst of crying around nights 3 to 5, just before things settle.
  • Nights improving while naps and early mornings lag by 1 to 2 weeks.
  • A short relapse around days 10 to 14.

Signs of real progress: less time to fall asleep, fewer or shorter wakings, or crying that is less intense. If any of these is trending the right way, keep going. Typical timelines are in how long sleep training takes.

Other things to check

  • The room: light leaking in at 5 a.m., white noise on a timer that switches off, a room that is too warm.
  • New skills: rolling, sitting, pulling to stand. Give plenty of daytime practice; see the 8 to 10 month regression.
  • Checks that stimulate: if your baby cries harder after each visit, make checks shorter and less frequent.
  • Different rules by day and night, or between caregivers.
  • Too many crutches at once: a pacifier that needs replacing eight times a night is a sleep association too.

Should I switch methods?

Only after one method has had a fair trial with the foundations in place: 7 consistent nights for check-based methods, 14 for gradual ones. If you switch, switch once, to a method chosen for a reason, and hold it for a week. Changing methods every two nights teaches a baby that persistence changes the rules.

Should I stop?

Pause if your baby is ill, if your pediatrician has concerns, or if a major disruption is under way. Wait until things are stable, then restart from night one. A clean restart works better than a half-continued plan. If the constant waking is the main problem, waking every 2 hours covers the common causes by age.

When to get help

If you have been consistent for 7 to 10 nights, the checks above have not revealed the cause, and your pediatrician has ruled out a medical issue, the problem is usually in the details of the plan: timing that is off by 20 minutes, a feed in the wrong place, a response that works against this baby’s temperament. Frequently asked

How long should I try sleep training before giving up?

Give check-based methods 5 to 7 fully consistent nights and gradual methods 10 to 14. If nothing has improved by then, look for the cause before either giving up or switching methods.

Why is sleep training getting worse instead of better?

A temporary worsening around nights 2 to 5 is common and usually comes just before improvement. If it keeps getting worse beyond a week, the usual reasons are inconsistent responses, an overtired or under-tired schedule, or illness.

Why did sleep training work and then stop?

Illness, travel, teething, a developmental leap or a nap transition can disrupt sleep, and the old habits can return if the response changes during that time. Going back to your original method usually restores sleep within a few nights.

Can some babies not be sleep trained?

Nearly all healthy babies can learn to fall asleep independently, but not every baby responds to every method, and a small number have medical causes for waking. If a consistent, well-prepared attempt fails, see your pediatrician and get the plan reviewed.
