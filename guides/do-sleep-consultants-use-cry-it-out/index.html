Do sleep consultants use cry it out?

Some do, many do not, and most use a range of methods. Approaches run from full extinction (cry it out) to graduated checks, staying in the room, and bedtime fading. Research supports several of these, not only cry it out. Ask any consultant to describe night one in plain words, including how much crying to expect and what you will do.

The main approaches

ApproachWhat you doCrying
Extinction (“cry it out”)Bedtime routine, baby down awake, you do not return until morning or a planned feedMost intense, usually shortest
Graduated extinction (timed checks)You check in briefly at planned intervals that lengthenLess intense for many parents; still some crying
Parent in the room (chair or camping out)You stay beside the crib and move further away over daysUsually some protest; lasts longer overall
Bedtime fadingYou temporarily move bedtime later to when your baby is truly sleepy, then bring it earlierOften less; slower
Pick up, put down and other hands-on approachesYou comfort with touch or by lifting until calm, then return baby to the cribVaries with baby; can overstimulate some

What the research says

An American Academy of Sleep Medicine review of 52 studies found strong support for unmodified extinction, clear support for graduated extinction, and support for extinction with a parent present and for positive routines with bedtime fading. The review reported no detrimental effects in the studies it examined. A randomized trial of 43 infants aged 6 to 16 months compared graduated extinction and bedtime fading with a sleep-education control. Both methods improved sleep. Infant cortisol and maternal stress fell more in the intervention groups, and at 12 months there were no differences in emotional or behavioral problems or in parent-child attachment.

So the evidence does not require cry it out. Several approaches with less crying also work. What they have in common is consistency.

Why “no crying at all” is rarely realistic

When you change how your baby falls asleep, your baby notices and protests. That protest is communication, not harm, and it usually fades as the new pattern becomes familiar. A consultant who promises zero tears is either describing a baby who was nearly ready anyway or setting you up to feel you failed. A better promise is a clear plan for how you will respond.

What to ask

  • “Describe night one for my baby. Where am I, and what do I do when my baby cries?”
  • “How long would my baby cry before I go in, if at all?”
  • “Can you adjust the approach if it is too much for us?”
  • “What is different for a younger baby versus a toddler?”

Matching the method to your family

The best method is the one you will carry out the same way every night. Parents who cannot listen to crying from outside the door often do better staying in the room, even though it takes longer. Parents who find the in-between approaches confusing sometimes prefer a cleaner, faster method. A consultant should ask about this before writing the plan, not after night one.

Frequently asked

Is cry it out harmful?

In the studies reviewed by the AASM, no detrimental effects were reported, and a randomized trial found no differences in emotional or behavioral problems or attachment at 12 months. Many families still prefer other methods, which also work.

Can sleep training work without any crying?

Some babies make the change with very little crying, especially with gradual methods. Most protest at least a little because something familiar changed. Plan for some crying and decide in advance how you will respond.

Which sleep training method has the least crying?

Approaches with a parent in the room and bedtime fading tend to involve less intense crying but take longer. Less crying per night can mean more nights. Consistency matters more than which method you pick.

Do gentle methods take longer?

Usually, yes. Methods where you stay close or change bedtime gradually tend to take more nights than extinction or timed checks. Many families accept that trade for less crying each night.
