Can my baby and toddler share a room, and how do I make it work?

Yes. Most families wait until after the first 6 months, the minimum the AAP recommends for a baby sleeping in the parents’ room. The baby needs their own crib with only a fitted sheet, and siblings should never share a bed with a baby. Stagger bedtimes, use white noise at a low volume, and keep your toddler from putting toys in the crib.

Safety first

  • Separate sleep spaces. The NIH Safe to Sleep campaign lists a sibling sharing a baby’s sleep surface as a high-risk situation. Each child has their own bed.
  • A bare crib. The CPSC’s advice is “Bare is Best”: nothing but a fitted sheet in a crib, bassinet or play yard. For the first year, keep blankets, pillows and stuffed toys out.
  • Toddler-proof the crib. Toddlers like to share. Teach that the baby’s bed stays empty, and check it before every sleep.
  • Toddler-proof the room. Small toys, coins and batteries go somewhere your crawling baby can’t reach. Anchor furniture to the wall.
  • Cords. Keep monitor and lamp cords well away from both beds. The CPSC warns never to place corded cameras within three feet of a crib.

When to move the baby in

The AAP recommends your baby sleep in your room for at least the first 6 months. After that, the timing is up to you. Many families find it easier once the baby has fewer night feeds, because night feeds in a shared room wake the toddler. Some families move the toddler in with the baby; others the baby in with the toddler. Either can work.

Bedtimes

Babies usually need to go to bed earlier than toddlers, or at least sooner after their last nap. Two common setups:

SetupHow it works
StaggeredBaby goes down first in a dark, quiet room. The toddler’s routine happens elsewhere, then they go in quietly with a dim light.
TogetherOne shared routine when the bedtimes are close. Easier with two adults.

Will they wake each other

Sometimes, especially in the first week or two. In practice, many siblings learn to sleep through each other’s noises surprisingly fast. Things that help:

  • White noise at a low volume, with the machine across the room. A 2014 study found some infant sleep machines were loud enough at maximum volume to raise concern about hearing, so keep the volume down.
  • Space between beds, or a bookcase or curtain as a divider, anchored to the wall.
  • Pause before responding. A baby who fusses for a minute often resettles. Rushing in can wake both.
  • A plan for early wakers. A toddler clock can teach your toddler to stay quietly in bed until it changes color.

Sleep training with a roommate

If one child is learning to sleep independently, it is often easier to give them their own space for a short time, even if that means the toddler sleeps in your room for a week. Then move them back together. See sleep training while room sharing.

Naps

Naps are harder to share because the timing rarely matches. If you can, use a different space for one child’s nap, such as a play yard in a quiet room. See which products are safe for sleep.

When it isn’t working

If one child is consistently waking the other after a few weeks, look at the cause. Frequent night waking in either child is usually a sleep-habit or schedule issue rather than a room issue. Fixing it often makes room sharing easy.

Frequently asked

At what age can a baby share a room with a sibling?

The AAP recommends a baby sleep in the parents’ room for at least the first 6 months. After that, sharing with a sibling is fine as long as each child has their own safe sleep space.

Can my toddler and baby sleep in the same bed?

No. NIH Safe to Sleep lists a sibling sharing a baby’s sleep surface as a high-risk situation. The baby needs their own crib, bassinet or play yard with only a fitted sheet.

What if the baby keeps waking the toddler?

Use low-volume white noise across the room, put space between the beds, and pause briefly before going in. Many siblings adjust within a couple of weeks. If not, address the baby’s night waking itself.
