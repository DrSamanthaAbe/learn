How do I prepare my baby’s sleep for when I go back to work?

Start a few weeks before: choose a wake-up time that fits your workday, make sure your baby can fall asleep for more than one person, and practise the new daily rhythm with the new caregiver. Keep bedtime early enough to protect total sleep. Expect a few rough weeks; that is adjustment, not failure.

Work out the new day on paper

Start from the times you can’t move: when you need to leave, and when you get home. Then build the day around them.

Fixed pointWhat it decides
When you leaveWake-up time. Allow time for a feed, getting dressed and the trip.
Daycare or caregiver scheduleNap times on workdays.
When you get homeHow much evening time you have before bedtime.
Total sleep needHow late bedtime can be.

For sleep need, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 12 to 16 hours per 24 hours for babies 4 to 12 months and 11 to 14 hours for children 1 to 2 years, including naps.

Shift the wake time gradually

If your baby will need to wake earlier on workdays, move wake-up time 15 minutes earlier every few days. Open the curtains and get light in the morning. Keep weekends roughly the same so Monday isn’t a jolt.

Make sleep portable

The biggest change is often that someone else will be putting your baby down. That goes more smoothly if your baby can:

  • Fall asleep for more than one person
  • Fall asleep in a crib or play yard, not only on you or while feeding
  • Recognize a short, repeatable pre-nap routine

Practise with your partner or the new caregiver in the weeks before. Write down the routine, and share how your baby shows tiredness. See daycare naps and bedtime routines.

Evenings

Many parents want to keep their baby up later to get more time together. A little later is fine. Much later usually backfires: an overtired baby falls asleep less easily and may wake earlier. Instead:

  • Make the time you have count: bath, play and the bedtime routine are all time together.
  • Use mornings too, if your schedule allows.
  • If daycare naps are short, bring bedtime earlier on those days.

A study of more than 10,000 young children found that the more nights a week a bedtime routine happened, the better children slept. Doing the routine yourself is a reliable way to reconnect.

Feeding

Changes in feeding often come with returning to work: pumping, bottles with a caregiver, or more feeds in the evening and at night. These are feeding decisions to make with your pediatrician or a lactation consultant. Don’t drop night feeds to fit work unless your pediatrician agrees your baby no longer needs them.

Night waking and a working parent

  • Split the night. One parent covers until about 2 a.m., the other after, so each gets a solid block.
  • Protect the parent who has the earliest start.
  • Keep night responses short and calm.

Timing sleep training

If you plan to work on sleep, starting two to three weeks before your return gives you time to see progress without the pressure of early mornings. Starting in the middle of the first week back is usually harder. Expect an adjustment

New germs from daycare, new routines, and missing you can unsettle sleep for a few weeks. That is normal. Keep the routine, keep bedtime protected, and give it time before deciding something is wrong.

Frequently asked

When should I start preparing my baby’s sleep before going back to work?

A few weeks ahead is enough for most families. Shift wake time gradually, practise naps with the new caregiver, and settle on a short routine that anyone can follow on a workday.

Should I keep my baby up later so I see them after work?

A little later is fine. Much later often leads to an overtired baby, harder bedtimes and earlier waking. Make the bedtime routine your time together, and use mornings when you can.

Do I need to night wean before I go back to work?

No. Night feeds are a decision for your pediatrician based on your baby’s growth and needs, not your work schedule. Splitting nights between parents can help you both get enough sleep.
