Are crib bumpers safe, including mesh bumpers?

No. Padded crib bumpers are banned from sale in the United States under the Safe Sleep for Babies Act, in effect since November 12, 2022, and the AAP says no bumper pads in your baby’s bed. The law does not cover non-padded mesh liners, but the CPSC’s advice is still “Bare is Best”: nothing but a fitted sheet in the crib.

What the law says

The Safe Sleep for Babies Act was signed on May 16, 2022. Since November 12, 2022, crib bumpers are banned hazardous products in the United States, regardless of when they were made. They cannot be sold, made for sale, distributed or imported.

The law defines a crib bumper as any material meant to cover the sides of a crib to prevent injury. That includes:

  • Padded crib bumpers
  • Supported and unsupported vinyl bumper guards
  • Vertical crib slat covers

It does not include non-padded mesh crib liners. If you received a bumper as a gift or with a crib set, don’t pass it on to another family; the safest place for it is the trash.

What the AAP and CPSC recommend

The AAP’s parent site is direct: there should be no blankets, pillows, stuffed toys or bumper pads in your baby’s bed. Soft objects and loose bedding can increase the risk of entrapment, suffocation or strangulation. The NIH Safe to Sleep campaign says research links crib bumpers, and bedding other than a fitted sheet, to sleep-related deaths.

The CPSC’s rule of thumb is “Bare is Best”: nothing but a fitted sheet in a crib, bassinet or play yard. In its 2018 to 2020 data, the CPSC reported an annual average of 100 deaths, most associated with extra bedding such as pillows, blankets or comforters.

What about mesh liners

Mesh liners are legal to sell because the law’s definition leaves them out. Legal to sell is not the same as recommended. The CPSC’s guidance is still a fitted sheet only, and the AAP lists nothing else that belongs in the crib. If you already have one, the safest choice is to take it out.

The problems bumpers were meant to solve

WorryWhat to do instead
Head bumping the slatsBumps against the slats are usually just noisy. Check the crib instead: MedlinePlus advises slats no more than 2 3/8 inches apart, about the width of a soda can.
Arm or leg stuck between slatsAnnoying, and it wakes babies, but a stuck limb is easy to free. Go in, free it calmly, and leave. A sleep sack can make it less frequent.
DraftsDress your baby in layers or a sleep sack rather than adding anything to the crib.
Noise of head bangingMove the crib a little away from the wall and tighten the bolts. See head banging and rocking at bedtime.

Older and second-hand cribs

If you are using a hand-me-down crib, check it against current standards: no drop sides, slats no more than 2 3/8 inches apart, no missing hardware, and a firm mattress that fits snugly. MedlinePlus states plainly that drop-rail cribs are not safe for babies. Remove any bumpers, positioners or padding that came with it.

Once your baby can stand, anything in the crib can also become a step. That is one more reason to keep it bare and to lower the mattress early; see when your baby stands up in the crib.

Frequently asked

Are breathable mesh bumpers safe?

They are not banned, because the law excludes non-padded mesh liners. But the CPSC recommends nothing but a fitted sheet, and the AAP lists no bumper of any kind as part of a safe crib. The safest choice is no liner.

My baby keeps getting a leg stuck in the slats. What should I do?

Free it calmly and leave. A sleep sack can make it happen less often. Do not add bumpers, slat covers or padding; vertical slat covers are banned under the Safe Sleep for Babies Act.

Can I use bumpers once my baby is a toddler?

Padded crib bumpers are banned from sale in the U.S. whatever the child’s age, and a standing toddler can use any bumper as a step to climb out. Keep the crib bare at every age.
