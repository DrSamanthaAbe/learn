When and how do I stop swaddling?

Stop swaddling at the first signs your baby is trying to roll; that is the AAP’s guidance. Rolling usually happens around 3 to 4 months, but some babies start working on it as early as 2 months. Once rolling attempts have started, stop immediately and move to an arms-free wearable blanket. The unsettled sleep that often follows is the part a plan can fix.

Why the timing is not flexible

Once a baby starts to roll, swaddling increases the risk of suffocation and strangulation; a swaddled baby who ends up on their stomach cannot use their arms to push up or turn. The AAP therefore says to stop when a baby looks like they are trying to roll, not once they have managed it.

Signs of rolling to watch for

  • Rolling to the side, or rocking side to side on the back.
  • Lifting head and shoulders high during tummy time, or pushing up on the arms.
  • Kicking the legs up and over, twisting the hips.
  • Regularly breaking out of the swaddle.
  • Any roll in either direction, even once, even by accident.

When you see any of these, the swaddle stops that night. Both arms out, in a regular, non-weighted wearable blanket (sleep sack).

The safety rule here is fixed. What happens to sleep afterwards is not, and that is the part Actually Sleeping fixes: a plan for your baby’s age that turns the end of the swaddle into the start of independent sleep.

Why sleep often falls apart afterwards

For many babies, the swaddle was doing a lot of the settling. It dampened the startle reflex, which starts to fade after about 2 months, and it gave a snug, familiar feeling at sleep onset. Take it away and some babies flail, startle and wake more for a few nights. Others keep waking for weeks, because the swaddle was one of the conditions they needed to fall asleep, and nothing has replaced it.

The timing makes it harder. Dropping the swaddle often lands near the 4-month sleep change, when sleep cycles mature and babies who rely on help to fall asleep start waking more often. Parents then change several things at once (more rocking, a later bedtime, a new product) and the baby ends up with more sleep associations, not fewer.

What not to use

  • Weighted swaddles or weighted sleep sacks. The AAP advises against them; they can place too much pressure on a baby’s chest and lungs.
  • Positioners, wedges or rolled towels to keep a baby on their back. Nothing goes in the sleep space.
  • Any product that restrains the arms once rolling attempts have started. An arms-free sleep sack only.
  • Loose blankets as a substitute.

Keep placing your baby on their back for every sleep. Once your baby rolls both ways on their own, you do not have to turn them back over. More in safe sleep and sleep training.

Frequently asked

Can I swaddle with one arm out long term?

No. Once rolling attempts begin, the AAP says the swaddle stops and both arms must be free. Any one-arm-out stage is only for a baby showing no rolling signs at all, and it ends the day those signs appear.

My baby is 8 weeks and not rolling. Do I have to stop?

Not yet, but watch closely from here. Some babies start working on rolling as early as 2 months, and the swaddle stops the day they show signs. Keep placing your baby on their back every time.

Are weighted sleep sacks a good swaddle replacement?

No. The AAP advises against weighted swaddles and weighted sleep sacks for infants because they can put too much pressure on the chest and lungs. Use a regular, non-weighted wearable blanket.

How long does the swaddle transition take?

Some babies adjust within a few nights. Others keep waking for weeks because the swaddle was part of how they fell asleep. When sleep has not recovered, the cause is usually how your baby falls asleep, which is what a Actually Sleeping plan works on.
