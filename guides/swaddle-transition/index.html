When and how do I stop swaddling?

Stop swaddling at the first signs your baby is trying to roll; that is the AAP’s guidance. Rolling usually happens around 3 to 4 months, but some babies start working on it as early as 2 months. If rolling attempts have started, stop immediately and go straight to an arms-free wearable blanket. If they have not, you can take a few nights to transition, one arm at a time.

Why the timing is not flexible

Once a baby starts to roll, swaddling increases the risk of suffocation and strangulation; a swaddled baby who ends up on their stomach cannot use their arms to push up or turn. The AAP therefore says to stop when a baby looks like they are trying to roll, not once they have managed it.

Signs of rolling to watch for

  • Rolling to the side, or rocking side to side on the back.
  • Lifting head and shoulders high during tummy time, or pushing up on the arms.
  • Kicking the legs up and over, twisting the hips.
  • Regularly breaking out of the swaddle.
  • Any roll in either direction, even once, even by accident.

How to transition

SituationMethodWhat to expect
Rolling signs have appearedStop tonight. Both arms out, in a regular wearable blanket (sleep sack).2–5 unsettled nights, then sleep returns to baseline.
No rolling signs yet, baby about 6–8 weeks or olderOne arm out for 2–3 nights, then both arms out, then into a sleep sack.Milder disruption spread over about a week.
Rolling signs appear mid-transitionSkip the remaining steps; arms fully out now.Same as stopping at once.

Making the first nights easier

  1. Start at bedtime, when sleep pressure is highest, then carry it through to naps the next day.
  2. Keep everything else the same: routine, dark room, continuous white noise, a pacifier at sleep onset if your baby uses one.
  3. Expect the startle reflex. It starts to fade after about 2 months. Arms will flail for a few nights. That is normal, not a sign the change was too early.
  4. Give a moment before responding. A baby with free hands often finds them and sucks or settles. This is the beginning of self-soothing.
  5. Lots of floor time by day, so rolling both ways arrives sooner and your baby is comfortable moving at night.

What not to use

  • Weighted swaddles or weighted sleep sacks. The AAP advises against them; they can place too much pressure on a baby’s chest and lungs.
  • Positioners, wedges or rolled towels to keep a baby on their back. Nothing goes in the sleep space.
  • Any product that restrains the arms once rolling attempts have started. An arms-free sleep sack only.
  • Loose blankets as a substitute.

Keep placing your baby on their back for every sleep. Once your baby rolls both ways on their own, you do not have to turn them back over. More in safe sleep and sleep training.

The opportunity in it

Dropping the swaddle often lands near the 4-month sleep change. It is a natural moment to start putting your baby down awake. Actually Sleeping works with families from the newborn stage (foundations first, night feeds stay in place, and the 14-day guarantee clock starts at 10 weeks), and the swaddle exit is frequently the first step in the plan.

Frequently asked

Can I swaddle with one arm out long term?

No. One arm out is a short bridge of a few nights for a baby who is not yet showing rolling signs. Once rolling attempts begin, both arms must be free.

My baby is 8 weeks and not rolling. Do I have to stop?

Not yet, but watch closely from here. Some babies start working on rolling as early as 2 months, and the swaddle stops the day they show signs.

Are weighted sleep sacks a good swaddle replacement?

No. The AAP advises against weighted swaddles and weighted sleep sacks for infants. Use a regular, non-weighted wearable blanket.

How long does the swaddle transition take?

In practice most babies adjust within 2–5 nights when it is done all at once, or about a week with the one-arm approach.
