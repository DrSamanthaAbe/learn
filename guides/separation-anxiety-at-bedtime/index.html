How do I handle separation anxiety at bedtime?

Separation anxiety is a normal stage that usually starts between 8 and 12 months, peaks between 10 and 18 months, and tapers off in the second half of the second year. Handle it with extra connection during the day, a predictable bedtime routine, and a short, confident goodnight; then check in briefly and consistently rather than staying until your child is asleep.

Why it shows up at bedtime

Somewhere between 8 and 12 months, babies grasp that you still exist when you are out of sight, but not yet when, or whether, you will come back. Bedtime is the longest separation of the day, so that is where the protest lands. It is a sign of healthy attachment, not a sleep problem in itself.

By day: build the idea that you come back

  • Peekaboo and hiding games. They are practice for exactly this.
  • Short separations. Step out of the room, say where you are going, return cheerfully. Lengthen gradually.
  • Always say goodbye. Sneaking out teaches a child to watch you constantly.
  • Play in the bedroom at happy times so the crib is not only the place where you leave.
  • Fill the tank. Ten to twenty minutes of undivided attention before the routine reduces protest more than any trick.

At bedtime

  1. Same routine, same order, 20–30 minutes, ending in the bedroom.
  2. One goodnight phrase, said warmly and once. Then leave while your child is awake.
  3. If they cry, check in briefly: a calm voice, a quick touch, the same phrase, and out. The message is “you are safe and I come back,” not “I will stay until you sleep.”
  4. Do not lengthen the goodbye. Hesitation reads as a signal that something really is wrong.
  5. A comfort object can help, but only after 12 months. The AAP’s safe sleep recommendations cover the first year, and until then the crib stays empty.

What prolongs it

  • Staying until your child is asleep, or lying down with them, as a new habit.
  • Bringing back night feeds or rocking that had already ended.
  • Bed-sharing as a response, which the AAP advises against for infants.
  • Responding differently each night, so your child keeps testing which version they will get.
  • Moving to a bed. A crib is containing and reassuring at this stage.

One or two hard nights during illness or a big change are a different matter; comfort freely and return to normal afterwards.

Is this a reason to delay sleep training?

No. If your baby does not yet fall asleep independently, a plan built around this stage, with a parent close by at first and predictable returns, tends to lower bedtime distress because your child learns exactly what happens next. When to talk to the pediatrician

If the anxiety is extreme throughout the day, lasts many weeks without easing, follows a major upheaval, or comes with signs of illness or pain at night.

Frequently asked

At what age does separation anxiety peak?

According to the AAP’s parent site, it usually starts between 8 and 12 months, peaks between 10 and 18 months, and tapers off in the second half of the second year. Bedtime protests can resurface later at times of change.

Should I stay in the room until my baby falls asleep?

As a short, planned step that you fade over several nights, it can be part of a plan. As an open-ended response, it usually becomes a new sleep association.

Can I give my baby a lovey for separation anxiety?

After 12 months, a small lovey is generally fine. Before 12 months, the AAP advises keeping soft objects out of the sleep space.

How long does separation anxiety at bedtime last?

The stage as a whole runs for months. In practice, an intense bedtime phase often eases within a few weeks with a consistent response, though it can resurface at later stages.
