What should a sleep-training guarantee actually say?

A real guarantee is written in the agreement, not only in marketing. It names a measurable result (such as hours of night sleep), a time frame, when that time frame starts, what you must do to qualify, and what you get if the result is not met. If any of those five parts is missing, you have a slogan, not a guarantee.

The five parts of a guarantee worth having

  1. A measurable outcome. Something you can count: hours of continuous night sleep, number of wakings, nights in a row. “Better sleep” and “a happier family” cannot be measured.
  2. A time frame. Within how many days or nights.
  3. A start point. When the clock starts. The first night of the plan? A certain age? This matters most for young babies.
  4. Your side of it. What counts as following the plan, and how that is shown. Daily notes or check-ins are a fair way to show it; vague terms that can always be read against you are not.
  5. The remedy. What happens if the outcome is not met. A full refund, a partial refund, extra sessions, or continued support. Know which one before you pay.

Common wording and what it really means

WordingWhat it really means
“Satisfaction guaranteed”No defined outcome. Ask what happens if you are not satisfied.
“We’ll support you until it works”More support, not money back. Can be valuable, but it is not a refund.
“Results guaranteed” with no detailAsk for the written terms. If there are none, there is no guarantee.
“Most babies sleep through in a week”A claim, not a guarantee. Ask where the figure comes from.
Specific hours, specific days, refund namedA real outcome guarantee. Now read the conditions.

Fair conditions versus unfair ones

Every outcome guarantee has conditions, and that is reasonable. A consultant cannot be responsible for a plan that was not followed. Fair conditions:

  • Following the written plan consistently.
  • Reporting each day what happened, so the plan can be adjusted.
  • A minimum age, because young babies still need to feed at night.

Conditions that make a guarantee hollow:

  • Terms that can never be checked, such as “perfect compliance” with no way to show it.
  • Exclusions so broad that any normal event (a cold, a tooth) cancels it.
  • A remedy you only learn about after you ask for it.

Questions to ask

  • “Can I read the exact guarantee wording before I sign?”
  • “What does ‘through the night’ mean, in hours?”
  • “When does the clock start for my baby?”
  • “How is following the plan measured?”
  • “If it is not met, what do I receive?”

Get it before you pay

Read the full wording before you pay, not after. Save a copy. If something is unclear, ask for it to be clarified in writing.

No guarantee is not a deal-breaker

Many consultants do not guarantee outcomes, and many of them are excellent. What matters is that they are clear about it. The real problem is a guarantee that appears in advertising but not in writing.

How Actually Sleeping handles this: The guarantee is written into the Terms of Service, word for word: “We guarantee that your baby will sleep 8–12 hours through the night within 14 days of consistent, correct implementation of your sleep plan.” If that does not happen, the family receives a full refund. The 14-day clock starts at the baby’s 10-week birthday, so younger babies can begin with foundations first. Daily 1:1 check-ins are how the team sees whether the plan is being followed and working.

Frequently asked

Do sleep consultants offer money-back guarantees?

Some do, many do not. Ask to read the exact wording before you sign. A money-back guarantee should say what result is promised, by when, what you must do, and that a refund is the remedy.

What does ‘sleeping through the night’ mean in a guarantee?

It should be a number of hours, stated in writing. Without a number, you and the consultant may mean very different things. Ask before you start, not after a disappointing week.

Is it fair for a guarantee to require following the plan?

Yes. No one can promise results from a plan that was not followed. The fair version defines how following the plan is shown, for example through daily check-ins, so it can be checked.

Why would a guarantee have a minimum age?

Young babies need to feed at night, so expecting a long night stretch early is not reasonable. A minimum age, or a clock that starts at a set age, keeps the promise realistic.
