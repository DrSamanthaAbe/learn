Sleep at 1 month: what’s normal, what’s hard, and when to get help

At 1 month, sleep is still scattered and short, and evening crying often builds. The AAP says fussing usually peaks at about 3 hours a day by 6 weeks, then eases. There is no official sleep target before 4 months. This is also the start of the highest-risk window for SIDS, so safe sleep for every sleep matters most now.

What is changing in weeks 4 to 8

Your baby is waking up to the world. By 2 months, the CDC lists milestones such as calming down when spoken to or picked up, looking at your face, and smiling when you talk or smile. Those first social smiles make evenings more fun and also more stimulating, which is part of why the late-day stretch gets harder.

The body clock is still forming. Night and day begin to separate a little, but long, predictable night sleep is not expected yet. Tummy time also matters this month: the AAP suggests building up to 15 to 30 minutes a day, in short sessions, by 7 weeks. Awake, supervised tummy time is the counterweight to back sleeping.

What parents find hardest

  • The evening witching hours. Cluster feeding and crying from late afternoon are typical. A dim room, low noise and a calm adult help more than changing tactics every few minutes.
  • Catnaps that last one cycle. Short daytime sleeps are expected at this age. They are not a sign you are doing something wrong.
  • Waking when put down. Many babies startle on the transfer. Laying your baby down feet first and keeping a hand on the chest for a moment is a common trick worth trying; it is a rule of thumb, not a study finding.
  • Sleep debt in parents. Trade shifts if you can. Protecting one longer block of sleep for each adult is a practical rule of thumb many families use.

Safe sleep matters most right now

The NICHD Safe to Sleep campaign reports that most SIDS deaths happen between 1 and 4 months of age, and that 72% occur in months 1 to 4. Everything below applies to every nap and every night.

  • Back to sleep, on a firm, flat surface, with nothing in the sleep space. The AAP says the side is just as risky as the stomach.
  • Room-share without bed-sharing. Never sleep with your baby on a sofa or armchair.
  • Pacifiers reduce SIDS risk even if they fall out after your baby is asleep. If you breastfeed, the AAP suggests waiting until breastfeeding is established.
  • If you swaddle, stop as soon as your baby shows any sign of trying to roll. The AAP notes some babies start working on rolling as early as 2 months.

Night feeds

Feeding on cue at night is still normal at this age. How often to feed, and whether a longer stretch is fine, is your pediatrician’s decision based on growth. Do not stretch or drop feeds on your own.

When to talk to the pediatrician

  • Any rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher: the AAP says call immediately for babies 3 months or younger.
  • Crying that changes in sound, comes with vomiting, fever or poor feeding, or feels like pain. The AAP advises checking with your pediatrician first to rule out a medical cause.
  • Noisy, laboured breathing in sleep, or pauses longer than 20 seconds (call 911).
  • You feel depressed or close to the edge. Tell your doctor; it is common and treatable.

Foundations now, plan later

Foundations can start now: a predictable wind-down, a dark room for the longest sleep, and safe sleep every time. Frequently asked

Why is my 1 month old so fussy in the evening?

Evening fussiness is common. The AAP says it tends to happen between 6 p.m. and midnight and that fussing usually peaks at about 3 hours a day by 6 weeks before easing by 3 to 4 months. Check with your pediatrician first to rule out a medical cause.

Should my 1 month old be sleeping through the night?

No. There is no official sleep target before 4 months, and the AAP says regular sleep cycles do not appear until about 4 months. Short stretches and night feeds are normal now. Your pediatrician decides how often your baby should feed.

Is SIDS risk highest at 1 month?

The risk window is now. The NICHD Safe to Sleep campaign reports most SIDS deaths happen between 1 and 4 months, with 72% in months 1 to 4. Back sleeping, an empty flat surface and room-sharing without bed-sharing all lower risk.
