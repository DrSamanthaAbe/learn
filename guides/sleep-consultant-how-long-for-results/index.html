What the research shows

In a randomized trial of 264 mothers and children aged 6 to 36 months, an online program reduced the time to fall asleep and the number and length of night wakings. Improvements were seen by one week, with additional benefits by week two. A review of 52 studies for the American Academy of Sleep Medicine found that improvements from behavioral interventions were maintained for 3 to 6 months.

A typical shape

  • Before night one: intake, plan, and any changes to the room or schedule.
  • The first few nights: often the hardest. Protest is normal while your baby learns a new way to fall asleep. This is when thorough preparation beforehand matters most.
  • End of the first week: many families see shorter settling and fewer wakings.
  • Second week: nights usually consolidate. A short bump is common and does not mean the plan failed.
  • Naps: usually lag behind nights. Daytime sleep pressure is lower, so naps take longer to become reliable.

What speeds results up

  • A plan fitted to your baby’s actual schedule and sleep drivers.
  • Every adult responding the same way, every time.
  • Daily review of what happened and quick adjustments.
  • A healthy baby and two calm weeks at home.

What slows them down

  • Changing approach mid-way. Each switch resets the learning.
  • Giving in on a hard night after holding steady on easier ones. Your baby learns to protest longer.
  • Illness, travel, daylight saving time or starting daycare in the middle.
  • A schedule that leaves your baby overtired or not tired enough at bedtime.
  • Night feeds that are still needed. That is fine; your pediatrician decides them, and they stay in the plan.

Most of these are exactly what daily check-ins catch early. A stall that would cost a family a week on their own is usually spotted and fixed the next day.

Results by age

  • Newborns: foundations, not results on a clock. The goal is safe sleep, good feeding and a routine your baby starts to recognize.
  • Older babies: the trials cited here enrolled children 6 to 16 months and 6 to 36 months, and both found that sleep improved.
  • Toddlers: often a louder first few nights, because habits are older and toddlers test limits, then steady progress once every adult responds the same way.

How progress is measured

A simple log: time down, time asleep, each waking and how long it lasted, and nap lengths. Compare week to week, not night to night. One bad night inside a better week is normal. Frequently asked

How many nights does sleep training take?

It varies by baby, age and approach. In one randomized trial, improvements were seen by one week and grew by the second. Actually Sleeping guarantees 8–12 hours through the night within 14 days of consistent, correct implementation of your sleep plan.

Why are naps taking longer than nights?

Sleep pressure is lower during the day, so it is harder for a baby to fall asleep and stay asleep for a nap. Naps commonly lag behind nights. Actually Sleeping coaches naps until they are solid, with no fixed end date.

Is it normal for sleep to get worse before it gets better?

A short setback a few nights in is common. It often means your baby is testing whether the new pattern is real. Stay consistent and tell your coach; with daily check-ins it is spotted and handled straight away.
