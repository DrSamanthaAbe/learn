When and how do I night wean my baby?

Many healthy, well-growing babies can drop night feeds at some point in the second half of the first year; some need them longer. Timing is your pediatrician’s call. Weaning on its own rarely ends night waking, because a baby who feeds to sleep keeps waking between sleep cycles. Hunger feeds and habit feeds

Young babies need to eat at night, and nothing on this page applies to them. Never restrict or stretch feeds for a newborn or a young baby, and never night wean a baby whose weight gain is in question.

In an older baby, night feeds tend to look like one of two things. A hunger feed is a full, active feed at a fairly predictable time, after which your baby goes straight back to sleep, while daytime intake stays steady. A habit feed is a few minutes of sucking until your baby is asleep again, often every 1.5 to 3 hours, close to the length of a sleep cycle. Babies who get a lot of their calories overnight often eat less, or more distractedly, during the day. Many babies have a mix of both.

Whether your baby still needs night feeds, and how many, is a question for your pediatrician. Babies who were born early, or who have had feeding or growth concerns, often keep them longer.

If you are up several times a night and hoping fewer feeds will mean more sleep, the feeds are usually only half the picture. Actually Sleeping fixes the other half, how your baby falls asleep, with a plan written for your baby and your pediatrician deciding the feeds.

Why cutting feeds alone rarely works

Every baby surfaces briefly between sleep cycles, all night long. A baby who falls asleep feeding at bedtime surfaces and asks for the same thing to get back to sleep. Take the feed away without changing how your baby falls asleep, and you usually trade feeding for rocking, holding or crying. That is why so many parents tell us night weaning “didn’t work”: the feed was never the only thing their baby relied on to get back to sleep. See the feed-to-sleep association.

Generic weaning schedules also assume an average baby. Your baby’s age, growth, number of feeds, breast or bottle, and the way they fall asleep all change what a sensible plan looks like. A schedule that suits one baby can leave another hungry, or simply move the crying to a different hour.

If you are breastfeeding

Dropping feeds suddenly can leave you engorged or with plugged ducts. If supply is a worry, talk with a lactation consultant before anything changes. Your comfort and supply are part of how a plan is paced.

When feeds come back in

Illness, a growth concern, fewer wet diapers, or a baby who is clearly hungry during the day despite extra offers. Feed, call the pediatrician, and adjust the plan later.

Frequently asked

Can I night wean a 4 month old?

Only if your pediatrician agrees. Many 4-month-olds still need one or two night feeds. Falling asleep independently and night feeding are separate questions, and the feeds stay in the plan until your pediatrician says they can go.

Is it better to night wean cold turkey or gradually?

For most babies, gradual change is gentler on daytime appetite and on a breastfeeding parent’s supply. The right pace depends on your baby’s age, growth and number of feeds, which is why Dr. Sam sets it for each baby, and only after the pediatrician agrees.

Will night weaning make my baby sleep through the night?

Not on its own. A baby who needs help falling asleep will still wake between cycles and ask for that help. Weaning works when it is paired with falling asleep independently at bedtime, which is what a Actually Sleeping plan teaches first.

Will night weaning hurt my milk supply?

A gradual reduction usually lets supply adjust. If you have supply concerns, talk with a lactation consultant before anything changes, so that the pace of any plan can be set around your supply and comfort.
