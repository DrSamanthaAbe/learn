What are the red flags when hiring a baby sleep consultant?

Walk away from a consultant who bends AAP safe sleep rules, suggests medication or supplements for sleep, tells you to drop night feeds without your pediatrician, diagnoses medical problems, promises results without written terms, will not describe how much crying is involved, or pressures you to sign before you understand what is included.

Safety red flags (disqualifying)

  • Anything that conflicts with safe sleep. The AAP advises that babies sleep on their back, on a firm, flat, non-inclined surface, without soft toys, pillows, blankets or other bedding. It says weighted swaddles, weighted clothing and weighted objects on or near the baby are not safe, and that there is no evidence that commercial devices marketed to reduce the risk of SIDS do so. A plan that uses any of these, or that props or inclines a baby, is not a plan to follow.
  • Medication or supplement suggestions for sleep. Melatonin, antihistamines and “natural” sleep products are not a coaching tool. Questions about medicines go to your pediatrician or pharmacist. See is melatonin safe for babies.
  • Dropping night feeds on the consultant’s say-so. Whether a baby still needs to eat at night depends on age and growth, and that is your pediatrician’s call.
  • Diagnosing. “It’s silent reflux,” “it’s a tongue tie,” “it’s sleep apnea.” A consultant who hears snoring, breathing pauses or poor weight gain should send you to your pediatrician, not name a condition.
  • Sleep training a newborn. Newborn work should be foundations: safe sleep, feeding on cue, a simple routine. Not leaving a newborn to cry and not stretching feeds.

Honesty red flags

  • “Guaranteed” in the ads, nothing in writing. If a result is promised, the exact words should be in the agreement you sign.
  • Medical-sounding titles that are not medical. “Sleep doctor” or “pediatric sleep specialist” from someone who is not a physician or a sleep-medicine clinician. Ask what the credential is and who issued it.
  • “No tears, ever.” Most babies protest when something about bedtime changes. An honest consultant tells you how much to expect and what you will do about it.
  • Vague about the approach. If they cannot describe night one in plain words, you will be guessing when it arrives.
  • Reviews you cannot check. Only first names, no dates, no way to tell whether the reviewer was a client.
  • Claims that sound like research but have no source. Ask where a number came from.

Process red flags

  • No health questions at intake. A good intake asks about growth, feeding, reflux, prematurity and breathing.
  • The same plan for every baby of the same age. Then you are paying for a template, not a consultant.
  • Support that disappears after day one. Ask how often they check in, through what channel, and until when.
  • Pressure. “Only two spots left today” or a discount that expires in an hour. You should be able to read the agreement and sleep on it.
  • Telling you to stop asking your pediatrician. A consultant works alongside your pediatrician, not instead of them.

The green flags that matter

  • A plan written for your baby by a named person.
  • Intensive, bootcamp-style coaching before the first night and daily check-ins after it.
  • A measurable result guaranteed in writing, with a refund if it is not met.
  • An intake with real health questions and a willingness to say “check with your pediatrician first.”
  • A plain description of how crying is handled, matched to your limits.
  • Reviews from identifiable families, with dates.

Frequently asked

Is it a red flag if a sleep consultant is not a nurse or doctor?

No. Most sleep consultants are not licensed health care providers, and the work is behavioral coaching. It is a red flag if they act like a clinician: diagnosing, suggesting medication, or overriding your pediatrician.

Should a sleep consultant recommend a specific sleep product?

Be cautious. Products that incline the baby, weight the baby, or claim to reduce SIDS risk conflict with AAP guidance. A plain crib or bassinet with a fitted sheet is all a plan should need.

What if a consultant says my newborn should sleep through the night?

That is a red flag. Newborns need to feed at night. Early work should focus on safe sleep, feeding on cue and a simple routine, with night feeds decided by your pediatrician. That is how Actually Sleeping works with newborns.
