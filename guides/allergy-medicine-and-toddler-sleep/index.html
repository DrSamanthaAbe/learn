Do allergy medicines affect a toddler’s sleep?

They can, in both directions. Older antihistamines can cause drowsiness or, in some children, excitement, and should never be used just to make a child sleep. Newer ones can cause mild sleepiness. Decongestants can make it harder to fall asleep. Treated nasal allergies, on the other hand, can improve sleep. Choose with your child’s pediatrician.

A note on scope: Dr. Sam is a Doctor of Pharmacy and a licensed pharmacist, not a physician. This page explains in general terms how a medicine, supplement or remedy relates to sleep; it does not replace your child’s pediatrician. Check with your child’s pediatrician before giving your child any medicine, supplement or remedy, and follow the product label.

The short answer

Untreated nasal allergies disturb sleep, and the right treatment can help. The wrong product, or the right one used for the wrong reason, can make nights worse. The goal is to treat the allergy, not to sedate the child.

How each type of medicine relates to sleep

  • Older (first-generation) antihistamines, such as diphenhydramine, can cause drowsiness, the AAP notes. They can also cause excitement in children, and the drug label says diphenhydramine should not be used to make a child sleepy. CDC guidance says antihistamines are contraindicated under age 1. More in Benadryl and baby sleep.
  • Newer antihistamines are described by the AAP as non-drowsy, though they may cause mild sleepiness, especially after the first dose. Ask your child’s doctor whether one suits your child.
  • Cetirizine and levocetirizine. In 2025 the FDA added a warning about rare but severe itching after stopping long-term use, usually after months or years of daily use. If your child takes one daily, ask the pediatrician how long to continue and how to stop.
  • Oral decongestants can make children act hyper, feel anxious or have a racing heart, the AAP warns, and some can make it harder to fall asleep. Ask before using them.
  • Steroid nasal sprays are called the most effective treatment for nasal allergy by the AAP, which says they can help a child breathe more easily and may improve sleep.
  • Montelukast carries an FDA boxed warning for neuropsychiatric events. Listed effects include bad or vivid dreams, sleepwalking and trouble sleeping. The FDA says it should be reserved for allergies that other medicines have not controlled, and to stop it and contact a health care professional right away if mood or behavior changes.

How allergies themselves affect sleep

The AAP notes that a blocked nose makes it hard to sleep, and that poor sleep leads to daytime drowsiness and low energy. Saline rinses and sprays are a drug-free daily option the AAP mentions. Tracking whether night symptoms follow a season, a pet or dust exposure gives your pediatrician useful information.

What to ask the pediatrician

  • “Are these allergies, a run of colds or something else, such as enlarged adenoids?”
  • “Which treatment fits my child’s age, and is it sedating or stimulating?”
  • “How long should my child take a daily antihistamine, and how do we stop?”
  • “My child snores or breathes through the mouth at night. Should that be checked?”

The AAP advises using over-the-counter allergy products only with your child’s doctor’s advice. Bring a list of everything your child takes, including supplements.

What actually helps sleep instead

  • Treat the nose, not the bedtime: the right allergy plan removes a reason to wake.
  • Keep the bedroom simple: wash bedding regularly and keep pets out if they are a trigger.
  • Same routine and same bedtime every night, with dim light in the last hour.
  • Independent sleep: once your child breathes comfortably, the way they fall asleep decides how well they resettle overnight.

Frequently asked

Can I give my toddler an allergy medicine to help them sleep?

No. Treat allergies when your pediatrician says they need treatment, not to cause sleep. The drug label says diphenhydramine should not be used to make a child sleepy, and some children become excited instead.

Does cetirizine make toddlers sleepy?

Newer antihistamines are generally non-drowsy but can cause mild sleepiness, especially after the first dose, the AAP says. In 2025 the FDA also warned of rare severe itching after stopping long-term use.

Can montelukast cause nightmares in children?

The FDA boxed warning lists bad or vivid dreams, sleepwalking and trouble sleeping among possible effects. The FDA advises stopping it and contacting your child’s health care professional right away if mood or behavior changes.

Do nasal allergies cause poor sleep?

Yes. The AAP lists difficulty sleeping because it is hard to breathe through the nose, and drowsiness and low energy from poor sleep. It notes steroid nasal sprays may improve sleep. Ask your pediatrician which treatment fits.
