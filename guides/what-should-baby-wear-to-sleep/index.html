What should my baby wear to sleep, and what TOG sleep sack do I need?

Dress your baby in no more than one layer more than you would wear to be comfortable in the same room, which is the AAP’s rule. No hat indoors and no loose blankets. Watch for overheating: sweating, flushed skin, or a hot chest. TOG is a sleep-sack warmth rating; choose one that, with the clothes underneath, adds up to about one layer more than you.

The AAP’s rule of thumb

The AAP’s parent site says your baby only needs one more layer than you would wear in the same environment to be comfortable. Dressing your baby in layers is safer than using blankets, and the AAP advises keeping the room at a comfortable temperature.

Neither the AAP’s parent site nor the NIH Safe to Sleep campaign gives a specific room temperature. The guidance is about how your baby feels, not a number on the thermostat.

Signs your baby is too hot

The NIH Safe to Sleep campaign lists:

  • Sweating
  • Flushed, red or hot skin
  • A chest that feels hot to the touch

Check the back of the neck or the chest, not the hands and feet. If your baby is sweaty, remove a layer.

Hats and head covering

The AAP says not to put a hat on your baby indoors once you are home from the hospital; hats are for the first hours after birth or the NICU. Safe to Sleep adds that a baby’s head and face should stay uncovered during sleep.

If you are checking layers because your baby keeps waking, temperature is worth getting right, but it is rarely the whole story. The waking itself is what Actually Sleeping fixes.

What TOG means

TOG stands for thermal overall grade. It is a warmth rating that manufacturers put on sleep sacks and swaddles: a higher number means a warmer, thicker sack. TOG is not part of the AAP’s guidance, and brands differ in how they rate and what room temperatures they suggest. Use the maker’s chart as a starting point, then adjust to your baby.

Putting it together

ComfortableLight long sleevesA long-sleeve sleepsuit and a light-to-medium sleep sack
CoolA sweaterA long-sleeve sleepsuit and a warmer sleep sack

These are examples of the one-extra-layer rule, not fixed requirements. Your baby’s skin is the real guide. Hands and feet often feel cool even when a baby is warm enough, because they are small and exposed; go by the chest and neck.

Things to avoid

  • Loose blankets and quilts. Keep them out of the sleep space for the first year.
  • Weighted sleep sacks or swaddles. The AAP says weighted products on or near a baby are not safe. See weighted sleep sacks.
  • Swaddling a baby who is trying to roll. Stop swaddling at the first signs of rolling and move to an arms-out sleep sack.
  • Hoods, hats and thick fleece in a warm room.
  • Heaters or electric blankets near the crib.

When the question is really about waking

Many parents start comparing TOG ratings because their baby keeps waking, and wonder if the baby is cold or hot. A baby who is too warm or too cold may wake more, so it is worth getting right. Frequently asked

What temperature should a baby’s room be?

The AAP and NIH do not give a specific number. They advise a comfortable room temperature and dressing your baby in one layer more than you would wear, then checking for sweating, flushed skin, or a hot chest.

What TOG sleep sack should I use?

TOG is a manufacturer’s warmth rating, not an AAP standard, and brands differ. Use the maker’s chart as a starting point, then choose clothing plus sack that adds up to about one layer more than you are wearing.

Should my baby wear a hat to sleep?

No. The AAP says not to put a hat on your baby indoors once you are home from the hospital. Hats can make a baby too hot, and the head and face should stay uncovered during sleep.

My baby’s hands are cold. Do they need another layer?

Not necessarily. Hands and feet often feel cool. Check the chest or the back of the neck instead. If those feel comfortable and not sweaty, your baby is dressed warmly enough.
