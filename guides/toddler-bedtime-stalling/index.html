My toddler stalls at bedtime with one more book, drink or hug. How do I stop it?

Build the usual requests into the routine before lights out, then hold a calm, boring limit. Offer the drink, potty, extra hug and small choices during the routine, use a picture chart so the end is predictable, and answer later requests with the same short phrase. Also check bedtime isn’t too early, because stalling is common when a toddler isn’t tired yet.

Why toddlers stall

Stalling is normal toddler behavior. Your child is learning that words can change what happens, bedtime means separation from you, and every extra request is a chance to stay up a little longer. Sometimes there is a real need underneath, such as thirst, fear or a full bladder. Often there isn’t. The goal is to meet the real needs before lights out, so the rest can get a simple, calm no.

Step 1: check the timing

A toddler who isn’t tired will fill the time somehow. The AASM recommends 11 to 14 hours of sleep per 24 hours for children 1 to 2 years old, and 10 to 13 hours for 3 to 5 year olds, including naps. If your toddler naps long or late, and then takes an hour to fall asleep, bedtime may be too early for now.

  • Shift bedtime 15 to 30 minutes later, or cap the nap, and see if stalling eases.
  • For a gradual approach, see bedtime fading.

Step 2: front-load the requests

Write down every request you hear after lights out for a few nights. Then put each one inside the routine:

After lights outMove it into the routine
“I’m thirsty”A drink of water during teeth brushing. A small cup by the bed if you like.
“I need to pee”A last potty right before the book.
“One more book”Agree the number before you start. Your child picks which ones.
“One more hug”A deliberate big hug and kiss as the very last step, every night.
“Lie with me”A few minutes of cuddle time during the book, not after lights out.

Step 3: make the end visible

  • A picture chart of the routine lets your toddler see what comes next and that the last picture is sleep.
  • A timer for book time shifts the argument from you to the timer.
  • Small choices give a sense of control: which pajamas, which cup, which song.
  • One “pass”. Some families give a single ticket that buys one request after lights out. If it isn’t used, it can be traded for a small reward in the morning.

Step 4: hold the limit, kindly

After the last hug, use the same short phrase every time: “It’s sleep time now. I love you. See you in the morning.” Then leave. If your child calls out with a new request, repeat the phrase calmly and briefly. Long explanations and negotiation tell your toddler the door is still open. A consistent, boring response tells them it isn’t.

Expect stalling to get a little worse for a few nights when you first hold the line. That is normal and usually passes when the rule stays the same.

When it’s more than stalling

  • Real fear of the dark or of being alone deserves comfort and a plan. See fear of the dark and separation anxiety at bedtime.
  • Getting out of bed repeatedly is its own problem. See when your toddler won’t stay in bed.
  • Loud snoring, gasping, or very restless sleep are worth raising with your pediatrician. See toddler snoring.

Frequently asked

How many books should I read at bedtime?

Pick a number that fits your routine, often one to three, and agree it before you start. Letting your toddler choose which books gives them a sense of control without adding more time.

Is my toddler really thirsty at bedtime?

Sometimes. Offer a drink during the routine, and a small cup of water by the bed if you like. After that, a request for water is usually a request for more time with you.

Should I ignore my toddler’s requests after lights out?

Respond briefly and calmly with the same short phrase each time rather than ignoring or negotiating. Check that real needs like a potty trip were met in the routine first.
