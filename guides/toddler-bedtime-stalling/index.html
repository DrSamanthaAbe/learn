Why toddlers stall

Stalling is normal toddler behavior. Your child is learning that words can change what happens, bedtime means separation from you, and every extra request is a chance to stay up a little longer. Sometimes there is a real need underneath, such as thirst, fear or a full bladder. Often there isn’t. And because the requests are so reasonable, one more hug, one more drink, they are hard to say no to.

A toddler who is not tired will fill the time

The AASM recommends 11 to 14 hours of sleep per 24 hours for children 1 to 2 years old, and 10 to 13 hours for 3 to 5 year olds, including naps. Those are wide ranges, and children sit at different points in them. A toddler with a long or late nap who is put down at a bedtime meant for a child who needs more sleep has an hour to spend, and they will spend it asking for things. No bedtime script works on a child who is not ready to sleep.

Why stalling grows

  • Negotiation teaches negotiation. When a request sometimes works, your toddler learns to keep asking. Long explanations tell them the door is still open.
  • The limit changes. Two books one night, four the next; one parent stays, the other leaves. Your child keeps testing which version they will get.
  • Real needs are met after lights out. If the last drink or potty trip happens after the goodnight, every night has a reason to call you back.
  • Your presence becomes part of falling asleep. “Lie with me” turns into a nightly requirement, and then into a request at every night waking too.

Expect stalling to get louder for a while whenever the rules change. That is normal, and it is the point where most families give in, which teaches the toddler that protest works.

Why generic scripts often fail

Charts, tickets and stock phrases from the internet are built for an average toddler. Whether they work for yours depends on your child’s age, temperament, nap, bedtime and, most of all, on two tired parents doing exactly the same thing every night. Frequently asked

How many books should I read at bedtime?

The number matters less than it being the same every night. When the count changes with your child’s protest, stalling grows. In a plan, the routine, including the number of books, is written down so both parents hold it the same way.

Is my toddler really thirsty at bedtime?

Sometimes. Real needs like a drink or a potty trip belong inside the routine, before the goodnight. After that, a request for water is usually a request for more time with you.

Should I ignore my toddler’s requests after lights out?

Ignoring and negotiating both tend to keep stalling going. What works is a response that is the same every time, set for your child’s age and temperament. Dr. Sam writes that response into the plan, and daily check-ins follow while it settles.
