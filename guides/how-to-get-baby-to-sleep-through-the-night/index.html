How do I get my baby to sleep through the night?

Babies sleep through the night when three things line up: they fall asleep at bedtime on their own, their daytime schedule and calories suit their age, and they no longer need night feeds. Timing varies widely. In one study of 388 babies, 28 to 57 percent of healthy 6- and 12-month-olds were not yet sleeping 6 or 8 hours straight. Before about 4 months, night waking to feed is normal.

Your pediatrician is the authority on your child’s health. Confirm growth, night-feed needs and any medical concerns with them before changing how your baby sleeps.

What “through the night” means

Researchers often define it as 6 or 8 hours of uninterrupted sleep. Parents usually mean 10 to 12 hours. In a Canadian study of 388 babies, 28 to 57 percent of 6- and 12-month-olds did not sleep through by those definitions, and sleeping through was not linked to their later development. Every baby wakes briefly between sleep cycles all night long; a baby who “sleeps through” wakes too, and goes back to sleep without help. See how infant sleep cycles develop.

The five things that make it happen

  1. Falling asleep independently at bedtime. This is the largest factor. A baby who is fed or rocked to sleep expects the same at each brief waking between sleep cycles, which in infancy last about 50 minutes. Put your baby down fully awake after the routine.
  2. The right schedule. A consistent morning wake time, age-appropriate wake windows, enough daytime sleep, and a bedtime usually between 6:30 and 8:00 p.m.
  3. Daytime calories. Full feeds every 2.5 to 4 hours in the day, plus solids from around 6 months as your pediatrician advises. Babies who snack all day tend to make it up at night.
  4. A consistent response to night wakings. Decide which wakings are feeds and treat every other waking the same way each time, using one sleep training method.
  5. A sleep space that helps. Dark, cool, white noise, and safe: back, firm flat mattress, empty crib.

What does not work

  • Cereal in the bottle. The AAP advises against it because it can increase gagging or inhaling the thickened mix. Solids are started at about 6 months, as your pediatrician advises, for nutrition rather than as a sleep tool.
  • Keeping the baby up later. Overtired babies wake more often and earlier.
  • Dropping naps. Less daytime sleep usually makes nights worse.
  • Inclined sleepers, wedges or positioners. These are unsafe for sleep at any age.
  • Waiting past 6 months for a baby with a strong sleep association. Habits tend to deepen, not fade.

If your baby used to sleep through and stopped

Look for a cause: illness, a new skill such as rolling or standing, a nap transition, or a regression at 4 months, 8 to 10 months or 12 months. Keep your response consistent for 1 to 2 weeks. If new habits crept in during the disruption, a few nights of your original method usually restores sleep.

When it is not behavioral

Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, persistent mouth breathing, poor weight gain, eczema that itches at night or frequent painful spit-up are reasons to see your pediatrician before any sleep plan.

Frequently asked

At what age do babies sleep through the night?

It varies widely. The longest stretch often reaches about 6 hours by 6 months, but in one study 28 to 57 percent of healthy 6- and 12-month-olds were not yet sleeping 6 or 8 hours straight. Babies fed or rocked to sleep at bedtime often take longer.

Will a dream feed help my baby sleep through the night?

Sometimes. A feed given around 10 to 11 p.m. while the baby stays drowsy can push the long stretch into the parents’ sleeping hours for babies under about 6 months. If it does not reduce later wakings within a week, drop it.

Why does my baby wake at the same time every night?

Wakings at predictable times usually fall at sleep-cycle transitions. A baby who needs help to fall asleep at bedtime needs the same help at those transitions. Habitual feeds at a fixed time can also set an internal hunger alarm.

Is it normal for a 1 year old not to sleep through the night?

It is common, and it is usually fixable. Many healthy 1-year-olds no longer need night feeds, so waking is often a sleep association or a schedule problem. Confirm feeding needs with your pediatrician, then address the habit.
